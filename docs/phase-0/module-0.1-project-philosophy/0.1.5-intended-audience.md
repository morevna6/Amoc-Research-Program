> **Publication note:** This document defines the intended audiences of the AMOC Research Program and the level of responsibility the programme assumes when communicating with each of them. It does not imply that all project outputs will be equally suitable for every audience. Different publications may vary in technical depth while remaining connected to the same underlying evidence, methodology, and uncertainty.

---

## Intended Audience

The **AMOC Research Program** is designed for more than one audience.

This is intentional.

The Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation sits at the intersection of physical oceanography, climate science, data analysis, computational research, environmental risk, and public understanding. A research programme that documents only its final calculations would exclude many readers capable of learning from or critically evaluating the work.

At the same time, attempting to make every document understandable to every possible reader would weaken the scientific structure of the project.

The programme therefore adopts a **layered communication model**.

Some outputs will be highly technical.

Some will be educational.

Some will communicate research progress to broader audiences.

All should remain traceable to the same underlying scientific record.

The objective is not to make every reader consume every layer.

It is to allow different readers to enter the project at an appropriate level without creating contradictory versions of the science.

---

# 1. Primary Audience

The primary audience consists of readers interested in the intersection of:

* climate science;
* physical oceanography;
* data science;
* scientific computing;
* reproducible research;
* open science;
* scientific communication.

These readers may include both specialists and non-specialists.

The programme is particularly intended for people who want to understand not only **what an analysis concludes**, but also:

* where the data came from;
* how the relevant physical quantity is defined;
* why a method was selected;
* what assumptions were required;
* what uncertainty remains;
* how the result compares with published science;
* what the result cannot establish.

The project therefore prioritises readers interested in the **research process**, not only the final output.

---

# 2. Researchers and Scientists

Researchers in fields such as:

* physical oceanography;
* climate dynamics;
* Earth-system science;
* marine science;
* palaeoclimatology;
* environmental science;
* applied mathematics;
* climate modelling;

represent an important expert audience.

The programme does not assume that its work will replace formal institutional research or peer-reviewed scholarship.

For scientific readers, its value is intended to lie primarily in:

* transparent documentation;
* reproducible workflows;
* source traceability;
* explicit assumptions;
* dataset comparisons;
* methodological replication;
* structured literature synthesis;
* documented uncertainty;
* open analytical outputs.

Scientific readers should be able to inspect how a conclusion was produced rather than being asked to trust a final visualisation or summary statement.

---

## What Researchers Should Expect

Researchers should expect:

* citations to original scientific sources;
* dataset provenance;
* methodological transparency;
* explicit distinction between observation and inference;
* uncertainty documentation;
* version-controlled revisions;
* reproducible analytical methods where possible;
* acknowledgement of competing interpretations.

Researchers should **not** expect repository publication to imply peer-reviewed status.

Formal peer review, where it exists, will always be identified explicitly.

---

# 3. Data Analysts and Data Scientists

Data professionals represent another central audience.

The programme is intended to demonstrate how data analysis changes when the subject is a complex physical system rather than a conventional business dataset.

Relevant topics may include:

* data provenance;
* metadata;
* missing-data analysis;
* temporal alignment;
* multidimensional scientific datasets;
* spatial and temporal resolution;
* anomaly detection;
* time-series analysis;
* uncertainty;
* physical constraints;
* reproducibility;
* modelling;
* scientific visualisation.

For data analysts and data scientists, the project should demonstrate that analytical technique cannot be separated from domain understanding.

A technically correct transformation can still be scientifically meaningless if the variables being combined represent different physical quantities.

A statistically significant pattern can still be misleading if the time series is too short, autocorrelated, non-stationary, or physically misinterpreted.

---

## What Data Professionals May Gain

The programme may serve as an example of:

* moving from domain research to data selection;
* documenting data provenance;
* designing reproducible analytical pipelines;
* handling scientific uncertainty;
* distinguishing exploratory from validated analysis;
* integrating domain knowledge into statistical reasoning;
* choosing modelling methods based on scientific questions;
* communicating technical findings responsibly.

The aim is not to present climate science as merely another dataset on which familiar tools can be demonstrated.

The domain constrains the analysis.

---

# 4. Programmers and Scientific Software Developers

Developers interested in:

* scientific Python;
* research software;
* data engineering;
* workflow automation;
* version control;
* reproducible environments;
* scientific APIs;
* open-source research tools;

may also find the programme useful.

As the computational component develops, code should increasingly demonstrate:

* modularity;
* traceability;
* data validation;
* separation of raw and processed data;
* configuration management;
* reusable functions;
* testing where appropriate;
* documentation;
* reproducible execution.

The programme may eventually provide examples of how scientific requirements shape software architecture.

---

## What Developers Should Not Assume

Software quality alone does not establish scientific validity.

A perfectly engineered pipeline can reproducibly generate a scientifically invalid result.

The programme therefore treats software engineering as a supporting discipline within the research process, not as a substitute for scientific reasoning.

---

# 5. Students and Independent Learners

Students and independent learners are a major intended audience.

This includes people studying:

* climate science;
* oceanography;
* mathematics;
* statistics;
* data science;
* programming;
* Earth-system science;
* scientific computing.

The programme intentionally documents foundational learning before advanced analysis begins.

This allows readers to follow the development of understanding rather than encountering unexplained equations, datasets, or models halfway through the project.

---

## Educational Value

Students may use the project to explore connections among:

* scientific theory;
* observational systems;
* raw data;
* derived quantities;
* statistical analysis;
* physical interpretation;
* modelling;
* uncertainty;
* communication.

Educational documents should answer not only:

> “How do I calculate this?”

but also:

> “Why is this quantity meaningful?”

and:

> “Under what conditions is this calculation scientifically justified?”

---

## Educational Boundaries

The project is not a substitute for:

* formal university education;
* textbooks;
* laboratory or field training;
* supervised scientific research;
* specialist professional training.

Its educational role is complementary.

It provides an openly documented example of how an independent researcher approaches a complex scientific subject systematically.

---

# 6. Independent Researchers

The programme is especially relevant to independent researchers working outside traditional academic institutions.

Independent researchers often have access to:

* public datasets;
* open-source software;
* scientific papers;
* institutional reports;
* version-control platforms;
* online scientific communities.

However, access to these resources does not automatically provide:

* institutional supervision;
* laboratory infrastructure;
* formal peer review;
* research funding;
* specialist collaborators;
* large-scale computing resources.

The AMOC Research Program therefore aims to demonstrate a model of independent research that recognises both its possibilities and its limitations.

---

## Value for Independent Researchers

The project may provide an example of how to:

* create a research roadmap;
* build knowledge before analysis;
* classify evidence;
* maintain a reference system;
* document uncertainty;
* establish research boundaries;
* track project decisions;
* separate learning from claims;
* publish research progress openly;
* revise conclusions transparently.

The underlying principle is that independence should increase the need for transparency, not reduce it.

---

# 7. Open-Science Community

The programme is intended to contribute to the broader open-science ecosystem.

Readers interested in open science may be particularly concerned with:

* public documentation;
* source traceability;
* version history;
* reproducibility;
* reusable code;
* accessible data workflows;
* transparent corrections;
* methodological openness.

Where licences and technical conditions permit, the programme will seek to make research components inspectable and reproducible.

---

## Open Science Does Not Mean Unfiltered Science

Open access does not imply that:

* every dataset is equally reliable;
* every paper carries equal evidential weight;
* every analysis is valid;
* every interpretation deserves equal confidence.

The programme therefore combines openness with evidence evaluation.

Transparency makes scrutiny possible.

It does not remove the need for scientific judgement.

---

# 8. Science Communicators

Science communicators, journalists, educators, writers, and visual storytellers represent another intended audience.

AMOC research is frequently communicated through highly simplified narratives.

Common communication problems include:

* equating the AMOC with the Gulf Stream;
* treating weakening and collapse as identical;
* presenting model scenarios as observations;
* presenting uncertainty as ignorance;
* presenting uncertain risks as certainties;
* attaching specific collapse dates to evidence that does not support such precision.

The programme aims to provide communication resources that preserve critical scientific distinctions.

---

## Resources for Science Communication

Relevant outputs may eventually include:

* concise summaries;
* diagrams;
* annotated figures;
* interactive dashboards;
* explanatory articles;
* animations;
* glossary entries;
* methodological explanations;
* source lists.

These resources should provide accessible entry points while allowing readers to trace claims back to more detailed project documentation.

# 9. Climate-Interested Public

Members of the public who want to understand the AMOC without specialist training are also part of the intended audience.

Public interest in AMOC behaviour has increased because of discussions surrounding:

* climate change;
* extreme weather;
* tipping points;
* sea-level change;
* European climate;
* ocean circulation.

Public communication will therefore aim to explain the subject without assuming advanced mathematical or oceanographic knowledge.

However, accessibility will not be achieved by replacing uncertainty with certainty.

---

## What Public-Facing Material Should Provide

Public-facing material should clearly distinguish:

* what scientists have directly observed;
* what has been reconstructed;
* what models simulate;
* what models project;
* what remains uncertain;
* what is actively debated.

The goal is informed understanding rather than either reassurance or alarm.

# 10. Environmental and Climate Communities

People working or participating in:

* climate advocacy;
* environmental organisations;
* conservation;
* ocean protection;
* climate education;
* sustainability initiatives;

may also use project outputs.

The programme acknowledges that concern for climate and ocean systems can motivate scientific engagement.

However, advocacy goals will not determine analytical conclusions.

Evidence must remain capable of producing conclusions that are:

* expected;
* unexpected;
* inconvenient;
* uncertain;
* contradictory to previous project assumptions.

The research process must remain independent of the desired emotional or political usefulness of a result.

* climate-risk professionals;
* resilience planners;
* infrastructure analysts;
* coastal planners;
* policy researchers;
* environmental decision-makers.

However, this is a **secondary audience**.

The AMOC Research Program is not itself a policy-advisory body.

Any potential application to decision-making must distinguish between:

* scientific evidence;
* scenario analysis;
* uncertainty;
* value judgements;
* policy choices.

## Policy Boundary

The programme may explain:

* what evidence suggests;
* what uncertainty remains;
* which scenarios have been studied;
* which possible impacts have scientific support.

It will not claim that scientific analysis alone determines what policy should be adopted.

Policy involves considerations beyond physical science, including:

* economics;
* ethics;
* law;
* social priorities;
* political judgement;
* risk tolerance.

# 16. Consistency Across Audiences

Different levels of explanation should not create different scientific realities.

For example:

A technical document might say:

> An observed trend is sensitive to record length, natural variability, and methodological treatment.

A public explanation may simplify this to:

> Scientists can observe change, but the short observational record makes it difficult to determine exactly how much represents long-term weakening.

The language differs.

The scientific meaning does not.

This principle applies throughout the programme.

---

# 17. Accessibility

Accessibility is a project objective.

Where appropriate, the programme should reduce unnecessary barriers caused by:

* unexplained jargon;
* undocumented acronyms;
* assumed specialist knowledge;
* inaccessible figures;
* unexplained equations;
* missing context.

Potential tools include:

* glossaries;
* acronym dictionaries;
* diagrams;
* explanatory notes;
* layered articles;
* linked foundational modules;
* accessible captions.

Accessibility does not require removing technical depth.

It requires providing a path toward it.

---

# 18. Readers Are Not Expected to Trust the Author

The programme does not ask readers to accept conclusions because they are presented confidently.

Readers should be able to inspect:

* sources;
* datasets;
* assumptions;
* code;
* methods;
* uncertainty;
* limitations;
* alternative explanations.

The intended relationship between the project and its audience is therefore based on **traceability rather than authority**.

The desired response to a project conclusion is not:

> “The author says this is true.”

It is:

> “I can see how the evidence and reasoning led to this conclusion.”

---

# 19. Feedback and Correction

Open publication creates the possibility of external scrutiny.

Constructive feedback may identify:

* factual errors;
* misunderstood physical processes;
* methodological weaknesses;
* overlooked literature;
* coding errors;
* unclear explanations;
* inappropriate assumptions.

Valid corrections should improve the project.

The programme should therefore treat documented correction as a sign of functioning research rather than as reputational failure.

Scientific work that cannot survive revision is not strengthened by hiding its mistakes.

---

# 20. Audience Boundaries

The project is **not designed** to provide:

* personalised climate-risk advice;
* investment advice;
* emergency guidance;
* operational weather forecasts;
* legal advice;
* deterministic local climate predictions;
* authoritative policy instructions.

Readers requiring such services should rely on appropriate professional or institutional sources.

---

# 21. What the Programme Asks of Its Audience

Readers are encouraged to distinguish among:

* finished research;
* exploratory analysis;
* educational material;
* methodological development;
* project planning;
* hypotheses;
* published scientific evidence;
* project-generated conclusions.

The programme will attempt to label these categories clearly.

Readers should not assume that every file in the repository represents a final scientific conclusion.

## Closing Statement

The AMOC Research Program is intended for anyone willing to engage seriously with the evidence behind one of the Earth system’s important circulation processes.

Some readers will arrive for the oceanography.

Some for climate science.

Some for data analysis.

Some for programming.

Some because they are learning.

Some because they are concerned about the future of the climate system.

The programme does not require all of them to become specialists.

It does require that the science presented to them remains traceable, appropriately qualified, and honest about what is known and what is not.

The objective is therefore not simply to make AMOC research public.

It is to make the **process of understanding it visible**.
