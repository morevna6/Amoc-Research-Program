## Why This Project Exists

The Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation, commonly referred to as the **AMOC**, is a major component of the Earth’s climate system. It is part of a basin-scale circulation that transports warm upper-ocean waters northward and colder deep waters southward through the Atlantic. In doing so, it contributes to the redistribution of heat, salt, carbon, nutrients, and other ocean properties. Variations in the circulation can affect regional climate, dynamic sea level, marine ecosystems, and patterns of temperature and precipitation.

Changes in the AMOC would therefore not remain confined to a single current or a single part of the Atlantic Ocean. Its behaviour is connected to wider oceanic, atmospheric, cryospheric, ecological, and societal systems. This makes it relevant not only to physical oceanography and climate dynamics, but also to the study of coastal risk, ecosystems, food systems, infrastructure, and long-term resilience. The magnitude and direction of any specific impact, however, must be assessed carefully rather than inferred from simplified narratives.

Despite its importance, the AMOC remains difficult to observe, reconstruct, and predict. The RAPID observing system began continuous basin-wide monitoring at approximately 26.5°N in 2004. The array combines measurements associated with the Florida Current, wind-driven Ekman transport, and density-derived mid-ocean transport to estimate the strength and vertical structure of the overturning circulation. This observing programme has transformed scientific understanding of short-term and interannual AMOC variability, but a record beginning in 2004 remains short relative to the decadal and multidecadal timescales on which the circulation may vary.

Understanding AMOC behaviour over longer periods therefore requires the combined use of direct observations, hydrographic measurements, ocean reanalyses, palaeoclimate evidence, statistical indicators, and climate-model simulations. These evidence types do not measure or represent the system in identical ways. Each contains assumptions, spatial and temporal limitations, methodological choices, and uncertainty.

A trend inferred from a model ensemble, for example, is not equivalent to a trend calculated from a two-decade observational record. A proxy-based reconstruction is not interchangeable with a directly observed transport time series.

> **This distinction is central to the project.**

The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change assessed that the AMOC is **very likely to decline during the twenty-first century under all assessed emissions scenarios**, while expressing lower confidence in the magnitude and timing of that decline. It also assessed **medium confidence that an abrupt collapse would not occur before 2100**.

These calibrated statements matter.

“Very likely to weaken” does not mean that the timing, rate, mechanisms, or consequences are known with equal confidence. Nor does it mean that variability, weakening, resilience loss, threshold crossing, and complete collapse can be treated as synonyms.

Recent observational and modelling work continues to demonstrate why caution is necessary. The latest RAPID reporting describes a weakening trend across the available observational period while emphasising that longer observation is required to determine how much of that trend reflects natural long-term variability and how much is anthropogenic.

Other recent work has examined how natural variability can temporarily reinforce or offset an anthropogenic weakening signal. Scientific disagreement and changing estimates are not reasons to abandon the subject. They are reasons to examine methods, definitions, timescales, and uncertainty more carefully.

This project exists to conduct that examination through a **long-term, modular, transparent, and reproducible research programme**.

It does not begin with the assumption that the AMOC is stable, collapsing, or approaching a predetermined tipping date. It will not select evidence according to a preferred conclusion.

Instead, it will distinguish among:

* direct observations;
* proxy-based reconstructions;
* theoretical mechanisms;
* statistical early-warning indicators;
* numerical simulations;
* model projections;
* scenario-dependent impact assessments.

Where findings conflict, the disagreement will be documented rather than silently removed.

Where evidence is incomplete, uncertainty will be preserved rather than converted into false precision.

Where a conclusion depends strongly on a definition, dataset, filtering choice, baseline, model family, or statistical method, that dependency will be made explicit.

The project also exists to connect **scientific understanding with computational practice**. Data analysis cannot be scientifically meaningful when it is detached from the physical system that produced the data.

Before calculating trends or applying predictive models, the programme will establish foundations in:

* Earth system science;
* climate dynamics;
* ocean physics;
* thermodynamics;
* water-mass formation;
* Atlantic circulation;
* observation systems;
* the historical development of AMOC research.

Every dataset, equation, transformation, visualisation, and model should eventually answer five questions:

1. **What physical quantity does it represent?**
2. **How was it measured, reconstructed, or simulated?**
3. **What assumptions were required?**
4. **What uncertainty and limitations does it contain?**
5. **What conclusions can it legitimately support?**

Open observational datasets and climate-model outputs create valuable opportunities for independent research, but access to data alone does not create scientific understanding.

A reproducible script may still reproduce a poorly framed question.

A statistically significant trend may still be physically misinterpreted.

A sophisticated machine-learning model may still be scientifically unjustified.

For this reason, the programme will integrate:

* literature review;
* source verification;
* metadata management;
* data validation;
* physical calculation;
* statistical analysis;
* computational modelling;
* uncertainty assessment;
* versioned documentation.

Another purpose of the project is to improve the relationship between **scientific evidence and public communication**.

AMOC discussions are often framed as a choice between reassurance and imminent catastrophe. That framing removes the distinctions that matter most.

Natural variability, long-term weakening, critical slowing down, loss of resilience, tipping risk, and full circulation collapse are related concepts, but they are not interchangeable observations or conclusions.

The project will therefore avoid using dramatic language as a substitute for evidence. It will also avoid using uncertainty as an excuse for inaction or indifference.

Scientific caution does not mean that a risk is unimportant. It means that claims should remain proportional to the evidence supporting them.

Throughout the programme, every module will return to four recurring questions:

1. **What do we currently know?**
2. **How do we know it?**
3. **How certain are we?**
4. **What remains unknown?**

These questions will guide the project from foundational study to:

* literature review;
* dataset selection;
* data engineering;
* exploratory analysis;
* physical calculation;
* modelling;
* interpretation;
* scientific communication.

Ultimately, this project exists because the AMOC is both **scientifically consequential and incompletely understood**.

Its importance demands sustained attention, while its uncertainty demands methodological discipline.

The purpose is not to manufacture certainty where none exists, but to build a traceable body of work that can be revised as observations improve, datasets expand, methods evolve, and new findings emerge.

The **AMOC Research Program** will therefore function not as a single completed analysis, but as a living and openly documented research programme dedicated to:

* understanding the circulation;
* evaluating evidence concerning its past and future behaviour;
* documenting scientific uncertainty;
* developing reproducible analytical methods;
* communicating findings with accuracy, transparency, and appropriate scientific caution.
