## Research Objectives

The **AMOC Research Program** is designed as a long-term, modular, and reproducible investigation of the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation.

Its objectives extend beyond producing a single analysis or visualisation. The programme aims to build a structured body of scientific understanding, computational work, documentation, and public communication in which each stage can be traced back to its evidence, assumptions, methods, and limitations.

The project therefore operates across several interconnected categories of objectives:

* scientific understanding;
* literature and evidence evaluation;
* observational data research;
* methodological development;
* computational analysis;
* reproducibility;
* uncertainty assessment;
* interpretation;
* scientific communication;
* education;
* long-term research development.

---

## Primary Research Objective

The primary objective of the AMOC Research Program is:

> **To develop a scientifically grounded, transparent, and reproducible understanding of the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation by integrating physical theory, observational evidence, published research, computational analysis, uncertainty assessment, and scientific communication.**

This objective does not assume a predetermined conclusion concerning AMOC stability, weakening, resilience, tipping behaviour, or future collapse.

The programme instead seeks to establish what can be supported by the available evidence, how that evidence was produced, how reliable different conclusions are, and which questions remain unresolved.

## Scientific Objectives

The programme aims to develop the theoretical knowledge required to interpret AMOC observations and analyses correctly.

### Objective S1 — Understand the Earth-System Context

Develop a working scientific understanding of the Earth as a coupled system involving:

* the atmosphere;
* hydrosphere;
* cryosphere;
* biosphere;
* lithosphere;
* energy exchange;
* feedback mechanisms;
* interactions among Earth-system components.

This foundation will ensure that the AMOC is studied as part of a wider physical system rather than as an isolated oceanographic feature.

### Objective S2 — Develop a Foundation in Climate Science

Study the physical processes necessary to understand climate variability and change, including:

* Earth’s energy balance;
* radiative forcing;
* greenhouse processes;
* heat redistribution;
* ocean–atmosphere interaction;
* climate feedbacks;
* natural variability;
* externally forced change.

### Objective S3 — Develop a Foundation in Physical Oceanography

Build the theoretical background necessary to understand ocean circulation through the study of:

* temperature;
* salinity;
* pressure;
* density;
* buoyancy;
* stratification;
* convection;
* mixing;
* pressure gradients;
* Coriolis effects;
* geostrophic balance;
* Ekman transport;
* water-mass formation;
* ocean thermodynamics.

### Objective S4 — Understand Global Overturning Circulation

Study the AMOC within the broader global overturning system, including:

* heat transport;
* salt transport;
* freshwater transport;
* deep-water formation;
* intermediate waters;
* North Atlantic Deep Water;
* Antarctic Bottom Water;
* Southern Ocean connections.

### Objective S5 — Develop a Complete Theoretical Understanding of the AMOC

Investigate:

* AMOC structure;
* major components;
* driving mechanisms;
* upper and lower circulation branches;
* return pathways;
* volume transport;
* heat transport;
* freshwater transport;
* carbon transport;
* seasonal variability;
* interannual variability;
* decadal variability;
* longer-term behaviour.

## Literature and Evidence Objectives

Scientific analysis must be grounded in existing knowledge rather than beginning directly with data.

### Objective L1 — Build a Systematic Literature Base

Develop a structured collection of:

* peer-reviewed research papers;
* scientific reviews;
* institutional assessments;
* observing-programme documentation;
* dataset documentation;
* methodological papers;
* historical AMOC literature.

### Objective L2 — Classify Evidence by Type

Distinguish clearly among:

* direct observations;
* hydrographic measurements;
* proxy reconstructions;
* palaeoclimate evidence;
* reanalysis products;
* numerical simulations;
* climate-model projections;
* theoretical studies;
* statistical indicators;
* impact studies.

These evidence categories will not be treated as interchangeable.

### Objective L3 — Evaluate Competing Scientific Findings

Where published studies disagree, the project will seek to identify:

* differences in datasets;
* definitions;
* observational periods;
* model structures;
* forcing scenarios;
* statistical methods;
* filtering choices;
* assumptions;
* uncertainty treatment.

The objective is not to force agreement, but to understand the origins and implications of disagreement.

### Objective L4 — Identify Research Gaps

Document:

* unresolved scientific questions;
* observational limitations;
* methodological weaknesses;
* inconsistent definitions;
* uncertainty sources;
* underexplored datasets;
* possible opportunities for replication or methodological comparison.

## Observation-System Objectives

Before analysing AMOC data, the programme aims to understand how AMOC-related quantities are actually observed or estimated.

### Objective O1 — Study Major Observing Systems

Investigate observing programmes and technologies including:

* RAPID;
* OSNAP;
* MOVE;
* SAMBA;
* Argo;
* CTD observations;
* ADCP measurements;
* mooring arrays;
* satellite observations;
* hydrographic sections;
* reanalysis systems.

### Objective O2 — Understand Measurement Construction

For every dataset used, document:

* what quantity is measured;
* what quantity is inferred;
* instrument type;
* spatial coverage;
* temporal coverage;
* sampling frequency;
* preprocessing;
* derived variables;
* uncertainty estimates;
* missing-data characteristics;
* methodological assumptions.

### Objective O3 — Evaluate Cross-Dataset Compatibility

Before combining datasets, assess whether they are compatible in terms of:

* physical definition;
* coordinate system;
* spatial domain;
* temporal resolution;
* measurement methodology;
* reference depth;
* units;
* uncertainty;
* observational purpose.

Similarity of subject matter alone will not be considered sufficient justification for merging data.

## Data Infrastructure Objectives

The programme aims to build a transparent and reproducible computational foundation for scientific analysis.

### Objective D1 — Develop a Dataset Inventory

Create a structured catalogue containing:

* dataset name;
* provider;
* scientific purpose;
* variables;
* geographic coverage;
* temporal coverage;
* resolution;
* file format;
* licence;
* DOI or permanent identifier;
* access date;
* version;
* known limitations.

### Objective D2 — Establish Data Provenance

Maintain traceability from:

> **source → download → raw data → validation → transformation → analysis → output**

No analytical result should become detached from the data and transformations that produced it.

### Objective D3 — Build Reproducible Data Acquisition

Where technically and legally possible, develop scripts for:

* automated acquisition;
* version checking;
* integrity validation;
* metadata retrieval;
* local caching;
* reproducible preprocessing.

### Objective D4 — Preserve Raw Data Integrity

Raw source data should remain unchanged.

Cleaning, transformation, filtering, interpolation, aggregation, and derived variables should occur in separate reproducible stages.

## Analytical Objectives

The analytical phase of the programme will progress from descriptive investigation toward increasingly complex scientific methods.

### Objective A1 — Assess Data Quality

Evaluate:

* missing values;
* outliers;
* temporal gaps;
* spatial gaps;
* duplicated records;
* measurement uncertainty;
* discontinuities;
* changes in instrumentation or methodology;
* suspicious or physically implausible values.

### Objective A2 — Characterise AMOC Variability

Investigate variability across relevant timescales, including:

* seasonal;
* interannual;
* decadal;
* multidecadal, where the evidence permits.

### Objective A3 — Investigate Long-Term Change

Evaluate whether datasets support evidence of:

* trends;
* structural changes;
* regime shifts;
* changing variability;
* changing transport components.

Trend estimates will be accompanied by methodological and uncertainty considerations.

### Objective A4 — Conduct Physical Calculations

Where supported by appropriate data, investigate quantities such as:

* seawater density;
* potential density;
* Absolute Salinity;
* Conservative Temperature;
* volume transport;
* heat transport;
* freshwater transport.

Physical calculations will follow accepted oceanographic conventions and documented equations.

### Objective A5 — Apply Time-Series Methods

Potential methods include:

* decomposition;
* smoothing;
* autocorrelation;
* spectral analysis;
* trend estimation;
* change-point detection;
* variance analysis;
* lag analysis.

Methods will be selected according to scientific relevance rather than technical novelty.

## Early-Warning and Tipping-Point Objectives

AMOC tipping-point research will be treated as a dedicated analytical subject rather than assumed as the project’s starting position.

### Objective T1 — Understand Tipping-Point Theory

Study:

* multiple equilibria;
* nonlinear feedbacks;
* hysteresis;
* bifurcation behaviour;
* resilience;
* critical transitions;
* critical slowing down.

### Objective T2 — Evaluate Early-Warning Indicators

Where scientifically justified, investigate indicators including:

* lag-1 autocorrelation;
* variance;
* recovery behaviour;
* distributional changes;
* change-point signals.

### Objective T3 — Test Methodological Sensitivity

Evaluate how indicators respond to:

* detrending choices;
* filtering;
* window size;
* temporal resolution;
* noise;
* observational length;
* missing data;
* baseline choice.

### Objective T4 — Avoid Overinterpretation

Statistical early-warning indicators will not automatically be interpreted as proof that a physical tipping point is approaching.

Any such interpretation must be evaluated against:

* physical mechanisms;
* observational limitations;
* model evidence;
* alternative explanations;
* published literature.

## Computational Modelling Objectives

Computational methods will be introduced only where they address clearly defined scientific questions.

### Objective C1 — Develop Reproducible Statistical Models

Use statistical models to investigate:

* trends;
* variability;
* relationships among variables;
* uncertainty;
* reconstruction;
* anomaly detection;
* scenario sensitivity.

### Objective C2 — Use Machine Learning Selectively

Machine learning may be investigated for tasks such as:

* missing-data reconstruction;
* pattern recognition;
* dimensionality reduction;
* anomaly detection;
* surrogate modelling;
* classification of physical regimes.

Machine learning will not be included simply to add an AI component to the project.

A model must provide scientific or methodological value beyond what a simpler method can reasonably achieve.

### Objective C3 — Compare Models Rather Than Worship Them

When possible, compare:

* simple baselines;
* statistical approaches;
* machine-learning methods;
* physically informed methods.

Model complexity will need to justify itself through improved understanding, performance, robustness, or interpretability.

## Uncertainty Objectives

Uncertainty is a central research subject rather than a final paragraph added after analysis.

### Objective U1 — Identify Sources of Uncertainty

Document uncertainty arising from:

* measurement;
* sampling;
* reconstruction;
* preprocessing;
* model structure;
* parameter choice;
* statistical assumptions;
* natural variability;
* scenario assumptions.

### Objective U2 — Quantify Uncertainty Where Possible

Use appropriate methods such as:

* confidence intervals;
* uncertainty ranges;
* ensemble spread;
* sensitivity analysis;
* bootstrap methods;
* alternative methodological specifications.

### Objective U3 — Preserve Calibrated Language

Project conclusions should distinguish among:

* observed fact;
* strong evidence;
* likely interpretation;
* model-dependent conclusion;
* hypothesis;
* speculation;
* unresolved question.

## Reproducibility Objectives

### Objective R1 — Make Computational Work Reproducible

Analytical outputs should be reproducible from documented:

* data sources;
* code;
* environments;
* parameters;
* configurations;
* assumptions.

### Objective R2 — Version Research Outputs

Maintain version histories for:

* research documents;
* datasets;
* processing pipelines;
* analysis methods;
* figures;
* conclusions.

### Objective R3 — Maintain Research Logs

Develop and maintain:

* assumptions log;
* limitations log;
* decision log;
* research journal;
* dataset inventory;
* citation register;
* version history.

### Objective R4 — Separate Exploration From Validated Analysis

Exploratory notebooks may be used to investigate ideas.

Results presented as formal project findings should eventually be supported by documented, reusable, and reviewable workflows.

## Interpretation Objectives

### Objective I1 — Connect Statistical Results to Physical Mechanisms

A statistically detectable pattern should not be interpreted without examining its physical plausibility.

### Objective I2 — Compare Results With Existing Literature

Project-generated results should be evaluated against:

* observational studies;
* model studies;
* reconstructions;
* established physical theory;
* major scientific assessments.

### Objective I3 — Consider Alternative Explanations

Where several explanations remain possible, the project will document them rather than selecting one without sufficient evidence.

### Objective I4 — Distinguish Detection From Attribution

Detecting a change does not automatically establish why that change occurred.

The project will distinguish between:

* detecting variability or trends;
* attributing those changes to specific physical drivers.

Potential impacts of AMOC change will eventually be investigated as a separate stage of the programme.

Areas of interest include:

* European climate;
* North American climate;
* dynamic sea level;
* precipitation;
* agriculture;

* biodiversity;
* carbon cycling;

## Scientific Communication Objectives

### Objective SC1 — Communicate Findings Transparently

Every published result should clearly distinguish:

* what was measured;
* what was calculated;
* what was inferred;
* what remains uncertain.

### Objective SC2 — Avoid Sensationalism

The programme will not use dramatic framing to increase attention.

In particular, it will distinguish among:

* variability;
* weakening;
* resilience loss;
* tipping indicators;
* threshold crossing;
* abrupt transition;
* collapse.

### Objective SC3 — Communicate Beyond GitHub

Research outputs may be adapted for:

* the project repository;
* the author’s website;
* LinkedIn articles and research updates;
* interactive dashboards;
* static figures;
* educational animations;
* narrated scientific content;
* presentations.

Different formats may simplify presentation, but the underlying scientific meaning should remain consistent.

## Open-Science Objectives

The programme aims to make its research process inspectable wherever licences, data-access restrictions, and technical limitations allow.

This includes:

* public code;
* source attribution;
* documented methodology;
* dataset provenance;
* transparent limitations;
* versioned research documents;
* reproducible figures;
* correction of identified errors;
* acknowledgement of scientific disagreement.

Open science does not mean treating all sources as equally reliable.

Transparency must operate alongside evidence evaluation.

The programme is intended to evolve beyond its initial analytical stages.

Potential long-term objectives include:

* integration of additional AMOC observing systems;
* replication of published analyses;
* comparison of observational and model-derived AMOC metrics;
* development of reusable oceanographic analysis tools;
* advanced uncertainty analysis;

## Criteria for Progress

The project will not measure progress solely by the number of files, visualisations, models, or publications produced.

A research stage represents meaningful progress when it improves at least one of the following:

1. **Scientific understanding**
2. **Evidence quality**
3. **Methodological clarity**
4. **Reproducibility**
5. **Uncertainty characterisation**
6. **Physical interpretation**
7. **Documentation**
8. **Accessibility of scientific knowledge**

A technically impressive result that lacks scientific justification will not be considered a successful outcome.

## Criteria for Scientific Claims

Before a major project-generated conclusion is presented, the programme should be able to answer:

1. What evidence supports the claim?
2. How was that evidence produced?
3. What method was used?
4. What assumptions were required?
5. How sensitive is the result to methodological choices?
6. What uncertainty remains?
7. What alternative explanations exist?
8. How does the result compare with published research?
9. What does the result **not** establish?

If these questions cannot yet be answered, the finding should remain exploratory.

---

## Objective Hierarchy

The objectives of the programme follow a deliberate sequence:

> **Understand → Verify → Acquire → Validate → Analyse → Model → Interpret → Communicate → Reassess**

The order matters.

Modelling should not precede understanding of the data.

Interpretation should not precede validation.

Communication should not remove uncertainty discovered during analysis.

And conclusions should never precede evidence.

---

## Closing Objective

The ultimate objective of the AMOC Research Program is not simply to produce an answer about the future of the Atlantic circulation.

It is to build a research process capable of producing **defensible answers**.

That requires understanding the physical system, respecting the limitations of observations, evaluating competing evidence, using computational methods carefully, documenting uncertainty, and remaining willing to revise conclusions as knowledge develops.

The project will therefore judge its success not by whether it produces a dramatic conclusion, but by whether each conclusion can be traced back through a transparent chain of evidence and reasoning.
