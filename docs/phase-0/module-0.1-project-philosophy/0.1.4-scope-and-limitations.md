> **Publication note:** This document defines the present scope and known limitations of the AMOC Research Program. It establishes boundaries for scientific inquiry, data use, computational analysis, interpretation, and communication. These boundaries may be revised as the programme develops, but material changes should be documented through version history and project-status updates.

---

## Scope and Limitations

The **AMOC Research Program** is intentionally broad in scientific ambition but deliberately constrained in what it claims to establish.

The programme is designed to investigate the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation through a combination of scientific literature, observational evidence, physical reasoning, reproducible data analysis, computational methods, uncertainty assessment, and scientific communication.

It is not intended to reproduce the full observational, modelling, or institutional capabilities of a major climate-research centre.

Its purpose is instead to build a transparent and traceable independent research programme in which questions, evidence, methods, assumptions, limitations, and conclusions remain connected.

The distinction between **scope** and **capability** is therefore fundamental.

A topic may fall within the intellectual scope of the programme while still remaining beyond its current analytical capability.

Similarly, a dataset may be publicly accessible while still being unsuitable for a particular scientific conclusion.

The existence of data does not automatically create analytical authority.

# Part I — Project Scope

## 1. Scientific Scope

The programme focuses on understanding the **Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation as part of the coupled Earth system**.

Its scientific scope includes research into:

* Earth-system interactions relevant to ocean circulation;
* climate dynamics;
* physical oceanography;
* ocean thermodynamics;
* large-scale overturning circulation;
* Atlantic Ocean circulation;
* AMOC structure and components;
* AMOC driving mechanisms;
* water-mass formation and transformation;
* heat transport;
* freshwater transport;
* salt transport;
* carbon transport;
* seasonal variability;
* interannual variability;
* decadal and longer-term variability where evidence permits;
* AMOC observations and observing systems;
* historical development of AMOC science;
* current scientific understanding and debate;
* AMOC stability and resilience;
* nonlinear behaviour;
* potential tipping mechanisms;
* uncertainty in observations, reconstructions, and models;
* possible climatic, ecological, and societal implications.

The project will gradually build the theoretical knowledge required to analyse these subjects rather than assuming that familiarity with data-analysis tools alone is sufficient.

---

## 2. Geographic Scope

The primary geographic focus is the **Atlantic Ocean system** and the regions physically connected to AMOC behaviour.

Relevant areas may include:

* tropical Atlantic;
* subtropical North Atlantic;
* subpolar North Atlantic;
* Labrador Sea;
* Nordic Seas;
* Greenland–Iceland–Norwegian Seas;
* western and eastern Atlantic boundary regions;
* South Atlantic;
* Southern Ocean connections;
* regions monitored by major AMOC observing systems.

The project may also investigate climate relationships outside the Atlantic where there is sufficient scientific justification.

However, the programme does not assume that every remote climate anomaly is directly attributable to AMOC variability.

Teleconnections and remote impacts require their own evidence.

---

## 3. Temporal Scope

The programme does not impose a single fixed temporal window across all research questions.

Different components of AMOC research require different timescales.

The project may examine:

* subannual variability;
* seasonal variability;
* interannual variability;
* decadal variability;
* multidecadal change;
* historical reconstructions;
* palaeoclimate evidence;
* future model projections.

The temporal scope of each analysis will depend on:

* dataset availability;
* observational coverage;
* temporal resolution;
* methodological appropriateness;
* uncertainty;
* the physical process being investigated.

Direct observational records will not be treated as though they automatically represent centennial behaviour.

Likewise, palaeoclimate reconstructions and model simulations will not be presented as direct observations of the modern circulation.

---

## 4. Evidence Scope

The programme may use multiple forms of scientific evidence, including:

* direct observations;
* mooring-array measurements;
* hydrographic observations;
* Argo observations;
* satellite-derived variables;
* reanalysis products;
* proxy-based reconstructions;
* palaeoclimate evidence;
* theoretical studies;
* process models;
* coupled climate models;
* model ensembles;
* statistical reconstructions;
* peer-reviewed literature;
* major scientific assessments;
* official observing-programme documentation.

These evidence types will be classified according to what they actually represent.

The project will not collapse fundamentally different evidence types into a single category simply because they concern the AMOC.

---

## 5. Observation-System Scope

The programme intends to study major AMOC-related observing systems and measurement approaches, including where relevant:

* RAPID;
* OSNAP;
* MOVE;
* SAMBA;
* Argo;
* CTD;
* ADCP;
* hydrographic sections;
* moorings;
* satellite observations;
* ocean reanalysis.

Each system will first be investigated independently.

Before datasets are compared or combined, the programme will examine:

* scientific definition;
* variables measured;
* derived quantities;
* temporal resolution;
* spatial coverage;
* reference coordinates;
* methodology;
* uncertainty;
* missing data;
* version history;
* intended scientific purpose.

Dataset integration will require scientific compatibility, not merely technical compatibility.

---

## 6. Data Scope

The programme will prioritise:

* authoritative scientific datasets;
* openly documented datasets;
* datasets with traceable provenance;
* datasets with identifiable versions;
* datasets with appropriate metadata;
* datasets whose scientific methodology can be understood and documented.

Where possible, dataset records will include:

* provider;
* version;
* DOI or persistent identifier;
* access date;
* variables;
* units;
* spatial coverage;
* temporal coverage;
* resolution;
* known uncertainty;
* licence;
* citation requirements;
* processing history.

The programme may use both raw and processed scientific datasets.

However, derived products will not automatically be treated as equivalent to raw observations.

* data acquisition;
* data validation;

* metadata management;
* transformation;
* aggregation;
* statistical analysis;
* physical calculations;
* time-series analysis;
* spectral analysis;

* sensitivity analysis;
* uncertainty analysis;

* structured data formats;
* scientific visualisation tools.

Specific technologies are implementation choices rather than scientific objectives.

A method will not be selected merely because it is technically impressive.

## 8. Statistical Scope

Potential statistical work may include:

* descriptive statistics;
* distribution analysis;
* correlation analysis;
* trend estimation;
* autocorrelation;
* variance analysis;
* spectral analysis;
* anomaly detection;
* change-point detection;
* uncertainty estimation;
* sensitivity analysis;
* resampling methods;
* regression;
* time-series modelling.

Statistical methods will be selected according to the research question and characteristics of the data.

A statistically significant result will not automatically be interpreted as scientifically significant.

---

## 9. Physical Calculation Scope

Where supported by appropriate datasets and scientific understanding, the programme may investigate physical quantities such as:

* seawater density;
* potential density;
* Absolute Salinity;
* Conservative Temperature;
* volume transport;
* heat transport;
* freshwater transport;
* anomalies in relevant oceanographic variables.

Physical calculations should follow documented and accepted oceanographic conventions.

The programme will not invent alternative physical definitions merely to produce novel-looking results.

---

## 10. Tipping-Point Research Scope

AMOC tipping behaviour is within the research scope of the programme.

Potential topics include:

* multiple equilibria;
* nonlinear feedbacks;
* hysteresis;
* resilience;
* critical transitions;
* critical slowing down;
* statistical early-warning signals;
* sensitivity of tipping indicators;
* differences between theoretical, modelled, reconstructed, and observed evidence.

However, tipping-point analysis is **not the predetermined destination of the project**.

The programme will not assume that:

* the present-day AMOC is currently at a tipping threshold;
* an observed statistical signal proves an approaching collapse;
* a single indicator can establish physical loss of stability;
* a specific collapse date can be inferred without substantial methodological and physical justification.

---

## 11. Modelling Scope

The project may examine or implement:

* mathematical models;
* statistical models;
* idealised models;
* numerical simulations;
* reconstruction methods;
* anomaly-detection models;
* machine-learning methods;
* scenario analysis;
* sensitivity analysis.

Models may be used to:

* explore mechanisms;
* reconstruct missing information;
* compare hypotheses;
* test sensitivity;
* examine uncertainty;
* evaluate analytical methods.

Models will not be treated as observations.

Model outputs will be interpreted within the assumptions and constraints of the model that produced them.

---

## 12. Machine-Learning Scope

Machine learning is permitted within the programme only where it serves a scientifically defined purpose.

Potential applications may include:

* missing-data reconstruction;
* anomaly detection;
* pattern recognition;
* dimensionality reduction;
* regime identification;
* surrogate modelling;
* comparison with conventional statistical approaches.

Machine learning will not be added merely to make the project appear technologically advanced.

Where possible, machine-learning performance should be compared with simpler baselines.

Interpretability, physical plausibility, uncertainty, and generalisation will be considered alongside predictive accuracy.

---

## 13. Impact-Research Scope

Later phases may investigate possible relationships between AMOC change and:

* European climate;
* North American climate;
* regional temperature;
* precipitation;
* dynamic sea level;
* agriculture;
* fisheries;
* marine ecosystems;
* biodiversity;
* carbon cycling;
* weather extremes;
* infrastructure;
* energy systems;
* economic exposure;
* societal resilience.

Impact research will be treated as a distinct analytical layer.

The programme will not infer precise local consequences directly from broad AMOC change without appropriate regional evidence.

For example, a change in AMOC strength alone is insufficient to calculate a specific:

* crop-yield loss;
* insurance premium;
* economic cost;
* migration estimate;
* port disruption;
* mortality estimate.

Such conclusions require additional domain-specific datasets and methodologies.

# Part II — Project Limitations

## 16. Limitations of Independent Research

The AMOC Research Program is independently initiated and maintained.

This creates both advantages and constraints.

Independent work allows:

* flexible research direction;
* transparent documentation;
* rapid methodological experimentation;
* open publication;
* long-term continuity;
* interdisciplinary learning.

It also means the programme does not possess the same resources as major scientific institutions.

Current limitations may include:

* no dedicated research vessel;
* no independently operated mooring array;
* no original large-scale ocean-observation programme;
* no institutional supercomputing infrastructure;
* no laboratory infrastructure;
* limited access to proprietary datasets;
* limited access to restricted publications or software;
* limited capacity for large climate-model ensembles;
* dependence on externally produced scientific observations.

The project therefore relies substantially on datasets, documentation, and scientific work produced by established research communities.

This dependence will be acknowledged rather than disguised.

---

## 17. Expertise Limitations

The programme is interdisciplinary.

It intersects with:

* physical oceanography;
* climate dynamics;
* statistics;
* mathematics;
* data science;
* computational modelling;
* ecology;
* economics;
* risk analysis;
* scientific communication.

No independent researcher should be assumed to possess specialist-level expertise simultaneously across every one of these fields.

The project will therefore distinguish among:

* areas of established competence;
* areas under active study;
* areas requiring external scientific guidance;
* areas where conclusions should remain limited.

When a research question exceeds the programme’s current expertise, the appropriate response is further study, consultation, collaboration, or explicit limitation rather than unsupported confidence.

---

## 18. Observational Limitations

AMOC observations contain unavoidable limitations.

These may include:

* limited temporal coverage;
* incomplete spatial coverage;
* measurement uncertainty;
* missing observations;
* instrument failure;
* methodological changes;
* calibration uncertainty;
* derived rather than directly measured quantities;
* dependence on assumptions used to calculate basin-wide transport.

Different observing systems may also measure different aspects of the circulation.

A result observed at one latitude should not automatically be interpreted as a simultaneous measure of the entire Atlantic overturning circulation.

---

## 19. Historical-Reconstruction Limitations

Long-term AMOC behaviour cannot be directly observed before the modern instrumental era.

Historical investigation may therefore depend on:

* proxies;
* statistical reconstructions;
* historical hydrographic measurements;
* reanalysis products;
* palaeoclimate evidence;
* model-data comparisons.

These methods can extend understanding beyond the direct observational period but introduce additional assumptions and uncertainties.

Reconstructed AMOC indices will not automatically be treated as equivalent to modern direct transport measurements.

---

## 20. Model Limitations

Climate and ocean models are essential scientific tools, but they are simplified representations of the physical world.

Potential limitations include:

* spatial resolution;
* parameterisation;
* model structure;
* forcing assumptions;
* initial conditions;
* representation of mixing;
* freshwater processes;
* convection;
* atmosphere–ocean coupling;
* model drift;
* ensemble dependence.

Different models may produce different AMOC behaviour.

The programme will therefore avoid presenting one model result as a deterministic forecast of reality.

---

## 21. Statistical Limitations

Statistical analysis can identify patterns but cannot automatically explain physical causation.

Potential limitations include:

* short time series;
* autocorrelation;
* non-stationarity;
* filtering dependence;
* trend-method dependence;
* window-size sensitivity;
* multiple testing;
* low statistical power;
* nonlinear behaviour;
* measurement uncertainty;
* missing data;
* structural changes in observations.

The project will document these limitations where relevant.

---

## 22. Correlation and Causation

Correlation does not establish physical causation.

A relationship between AMOC-related variables and another climate variable may arise from:

* direct physical interaction;
* indirect interaction;
* common forcing;
* temporal coincidence;
* autocorrelation;
* shared trends;
* methodological artefacts.

Causal interpretation will therefore require physical reasoning and supporting evidence beyond correlation alone.

---

## 23. Detection and Attribution Limitations

The project will distinguish between:

### Detection

Identifying that a statistically or physically meaningful change has occurred.

### Attribution

Determining why that change occurred.

A detected AMOC trend does not automatically establish whether the cause is:

* anthropogenic forcing;
* natural variability;
* volcanic forcing;
* freshwater forcing;
* atmospheric variability;
* model or measurement artefacts;
* some combination of processes.

Attribution claims require additional evidence and appropriate methodology.

---

## 24. Prediction Limitations

The project may eventually investigate predictive methods.

However, AMOC prediction involves substantial physical, observational, and methodological uncertainty.

The programme does not currently promise:

* deterministic AMOC forecasts;
* exact collapse timing;
* exact tipping thresholds;
* precise regional climate outcomes;
* precise economic consequences.

Where forecasts or scenarios are investigated, they will be described according to their assumptions and uncertainty.

---

## 25. Tipping-Point Limitations

Tipping-point research presents particular interpretive risks.

Potential indicators such as increasing variance or autocorrelation may be scientifically useful, but they can also be sensitive to:

* preprocessing;
* detrending;
* window choice;
* temporal resolution;
* noise structure;
* record length;
* non-stationarity;
* external forcing.

An early-warning indicator is therefore not automatically equivalent to evidence that a tipping point is imminent.

Multiple lines of evidence should be considered.

---

## 26. Data-Integration Limitations

The project may eventually integrate multiple datasets.

However, datasets collected at different:

* latitudes;
* depths;
* spatial resolutions;
* temporal resolutions;
* observational systems;
* physical definitions;

cannot necessarily be combined directly.

Before integration, compatibility must be scientifically evaluated.

Technical success in joining two tables is not evidence that the resulting dataset represents a physically meaningful quantity.

---

## 27. Data-Access Limitations

Some potentially useful data may be:

* proprietary;
* restricted;
* temporarily unavailable;
* computationally expensive;
* poorly documented;
* subject to redistribution restrictions.

The programme will prioritise transparent and legally usable scientific data.

If important evidence cannot be accessed, that limitation will be documented.

---

## 28. Computational Limitations

Some scientific questions may require computational resources beyond those available to the project.

Potential constraints include:

* large climate-model ensembles;
* high-resolution simulations;
* very large multidimensional datasets;
* storage requirements;
* memory limitations;
* processing time;
* specialised scientific software.

When necessary, the project may use:

* reduced datasets;
* subsets;
* publicly processed products;
* cloud-based environments;
* simpler models.

Such compromises must be documented where they affect interpretation.

---

## 29. Reproducibility Limitations

The programme aims for strong reproducibility, but perfect reproducibility may not always be possible.

Potential obstacles include:

* changing external datasets;
* revised data releases;
* removed URLs;
* proprietary dependencies;
* external API changes;
* software-version changes;
* unavailable historical environments.

The project will mitigate these risks through:

* version tracking;
* persistent identifiers where available;
* environment documentation;
* archived metadata;
* source records;
* explicit dependency management.

---

## 30. Publication and Peer-Review Limitations

Documents published within this repository are **not automatically peer-reviewed scientific publications**.

GitHub publication provides:

* transparency;
* traceability;
* open access;
* version history;
* public scrutiny.

It does not itself provide formal peer review.

Project findings should therefore be described appropriately.

If work from the programme is later formally peer-reviewed, that status should be clearly distinguished from ordinary repository publication.

---

## 31. Communication Limitations

Scientific communication always involves compression.

A LinkedIn post, visualisation, animation, or public article cannot contain the same methodological detail as a complete analytical document.

This creates a risk of oversimplification.

The programme will attempt to reduce this risk by linking public communication back to:

* source documents;
* datasets;
* methodology;
* code;
* uncertainty;
* full interpretation.

Shorter communication formats should act as entry points into the research rather than substitutes for it.

# Part III — Explicit Exclusions

## 32. What This Project Is Not

The AMOC Research Program is not:

* an institutional climate-monitoring programme;
* a replacement for RAPID, OSNAP, NOAA, NOC, IPCC, or other scientific institutions;
* a climate prediction service;
* a weather-forecasting service;
* an operational early-warning system;
* a policy authority;
* a financial forecasting service;
* a commercial risk-rating agency;
* a substitute for peer-reviewed scientific literature;
* a platform designed to prove a predetermined AMOC-collapse narrative.

---

## 33. Claims Explicitly Outside the Current Scope

Unless future evidence and methodology justify revision, the project will not claim:

* that AMOC collapse is inevitable;
* that AMOC collapse is impossible;
* that a specific collapse date is known;
* that one early-warning indicator proves an approaching tipping point;
* that one observational array measures every component of the AMOC;
* that one model represents the true future state of the circulation;
* that correlation establishes causation;
* that observed AMOC variability can automatically be attributed to anthropogenic climate change;
* that natural variability automatically explains observed long-term change;
* that regional climate impacts can be calculated directly from AMOC strength alone;
* that machine learning can replace physical understanding;
* that statistical sophistication compensates for weak scientific assumptions.

# Part IV — Scope Management

## 34. Criteria for Adding New Research Areas

A new topic should be added to the programme only when it satisfies at least one of the following conditions:

1. It is necessary to understand AMOC physics.
2. It improves interpretation of AMOC observations.
3. It helps evaluate competing scientific explanations.
4. It supports a defined analytical objective.
5. It addresses an identified research gap.
6. It improves uncertainty assessment.
7. It improves reproducibility.
8. It supports scientifically justified impact analysis.
9. It materially improves communication or education.

Interesting topics that do not contribute to these objectives should remain outside the active project.

---

## 35. Criteria for Expanding Analytical Scope

A new analytical method should not be introduced solely because it is available.

Before adoption, the project should ask:

1. What scientific question does this method address?
2. Why is the method appropriate for the data?
3. What assumptions does it make?
4. What simpler alternatives exist?
5. How will its output be validated?
6. How sensitive is it to methodological choices?
7. What additional uncertainty does it introduce?
8. Does it improve understanding?

If these questions cannot be answered, the method should remain exploratory.

---

## 36. Criteria for Revising Scope

The scope of the project may change when:

* new datasets become available;
* scientific understanding changes;
* new research gaps are identified;
* technical capabilities improve;
* collaboration becomes available;
* existing assumptions prove inadequate;
* new evidence materially changes research priorities.

Material scope changes should be documented in:

* the relevant research document;
* `PROJECT_STATUS.md`;
* `CHANGELOG.md`;
* assumptions or decision logs once those systems exist.

# Part V — Interpretation of the Boundaries

## 37. Limitations Are Not Failures

A limitation does not invalidate a research project.

Scientific research is defined partly by the ability to identify what evidence cannot establish.

The programme therefore treats limitations as information.

A result may still be useful when:

* uncertainty is large;
* observations are incomplete;
* models disagree;
* attribution is unresolved;
* multiple explanations remain possible.

The appropriate response is not to hide those limitations.

It is to state them clearly and adjust the strength of the conclusion accordingly.

---

## 38. Scope Is Expected to Evolve

This document defines the current boundaries of a programme intended to develop over time.

Some limitations may decrease as:

* knowledge improves;
* datasets expand;
* analytical methods develop;
* computational resources increase;
* collaborations emerge.

Other limitations may prove fundamental.

The scope will therefore be reviewed periodically.

Changes should reflect scientific need rather than a desire to make the project appear larger.

---

## 39. Relationship to the Research Objectives

The objectives defined in [`0.1.3 — Research Objectives`](0.1.3-research-objectives.md) describe what the programme intends to investigate.

This document defines the conditions under which those objectives can be pursued responsibly.

The relationship can be summarised as:

> **Objectives define ambition. Scope defines relevance. Limitations define credibility.**

All three are necessary.

Without objectives, the project lacks direction.

Without scope, it expands without discipline.

Without limitations, it risks claiming more than its evidence can support.

---

## Closing Statement

The AMOC Research Program is intentionally ambitious in what it seeks to understand and deliberately conservative in what it claims to know.

Its scientific scope is broad because the AMOC is connected to many components of the Earth system.

Its analytical boundaries are strict because complexity does not justify speculation.

The programme will expand when evidence, methodology, and scientific relevance justify expansion.

It will stop where available evidence can no longer support a defensible conclusion.

That boundary is not an obstacle to the research.

It is part of the research.
