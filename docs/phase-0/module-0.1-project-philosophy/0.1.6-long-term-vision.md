> **Publication note:** This document describes the long-term direction of the AMOC Research Program. It defines an intended trajectory rather than a fixed list of guaranteed deliverables. The programme is expected to evolve as scientific understanding, available datasets, analytical methods, computational capabilities, and research priorities change.

---

## Long-Term Vision

The **AMOC Research Program** is intended to become a long-term, evolving scientific research environment centred on the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation and the Earth-system processes required to understand it.

Its purpose is not to reach one final analysis and declare the project complete.

The longer-term ambition is to develop an increasingly mature body of:

* scientific knowledge;
* literature synthesis;
* observational understanding;
* reproducible data infrastructure;
* physical and statistical analysis;
* computational modelling;
* uncertainty assessment;
* methodological documentation;
* educational material;
* scientific communication.

The programme should become more rigorous as it grows, not merely larger.

New datasets, models, figures, or publications should therefore be added only when they improve the programme’s ability to understand, test, reproduce, interpret, or communicate scientifically meaningful questions.

The long-term vision can be summarised as:

> **A living, reproducible, and openly documented research programme that grows with the evidence rather than toward a predetermined conclusion.**

---

# 1. A Living Research Programme

The AMOC Research Program is designed as a **living research record**.

Scientific understanding changes.

Observational records lengthen.

Datasets are revised.

Models improve.

Methods are challenged.

New papers modify previous interpretations.

Questions that appear settled may become uncertain again, while questions that were previously inaccessible may become testable.

The programme must therefore be capable of revision.

Its documents should not be treated as static declarations preserved merely because they were published first.

Where new evidence changes an earlier interpretation, the programme should:

1. preserve the earlier version;
2. document the new evidence;
3. explain why revision is necessary;
4. update the relevant interpretation;
5. record the change transparently.

Revision is not a weakness of the programme.

It is evidence that the research process remains responsive to evidence.

# 2. From Research Framework to Scientific Investigation

The programme begins deliberately with research philosophy and methodology rather than datasets.

Over time, it is intended to progress through several broad stages.

## Foundation

Establish:

* project philosophy;
* scientific methodology;
* evidence standards;
* literature-management systems;
* documentation standards;
* reproducibility principles.

## Scientific Learning

Develop foundations in:

* Earth-system science;
* climate science;
* physical oceanography;
* ocean physics;
* thermodynamics;
* thermohaline circulation;
* Atlantic circulation;
* AMOC mechanisms.

## Observational Understanding

Study:

* major observing programmes;
* instrumentation;
* transport calculations;
* observational uncertainty;
* differences among measurement systems.

## Evidence Synthesis

Investigate:

* historical AMOC science;
* current scientific understanding;
* competing interpretations;
* uncertainty;
* unresolved research questions.

## Data Research

Build:

* dataset inventories;
* data-acquisition workflows;
* metadata systems;
* validation procedures;
* reproducible preprocessing.

## Scientific Analysis

Conduct:

* exploratory analysis;
* physical calculations;
* statistical analysis;
* time-series analysis;
* sensitivity analysis;
* uncertainty assessment.

## Computational Modelling

Where justified, investigate:

* statistical models;
* numerical approaches;
* reconstruction;
* scenario analysis;
* machine learning;
* model comparison.

## Interpretation

Connect results with:

* physical mechanisms;
* existing literature;
* observational limitations;
* model evidence;
* uncertainty;
* alternative explanations.

## Communication

Translate appropriate findings into:

* technical documentation;
* research articles;
* visualisations;
* dashboards;
* animations;
* educational material;
* public scientific communication.

These stages should remain connected.

Later computational work should always retain a visible path back to the scientific foundations established earlier.

# 3. An Expanding Scientific Knowledge Base

A major long-term objective is to create an organised knowledge base around the AMOC.

This may eventually include:

* literature records;
* annotated references;
* scientific summaries;
* terminology;
* equations;
* derivations;
* observational methodologies;
* datasets;
* model descriptions;
* uncertainty records;
* historical research milestones;
* competing hypotheses;
* research gaps.

The value of this system will not depend only on how much information it contains.

Its value should come from how clearly the information is connected.

For example, a future analytical result should ideally be traceable through:

> **result → method → processed data → raw data → observational methodology → physical definition → literature**

This creates continuity between scientific learning and computational analysis.

---

# 4. A Reproducible Analytical Resource

The programme is intended to grow into a reproducible analytical resource rather than a collection of isolated notebooks.

Long-term analytical infrastructure may include:

* automated data acquisition;
* raw-data preservation;
* validation layers;
* reproducible preprocessing;
* reusable scientific functions;
* structured configuration;
* environment documentation;
* tests where appropriate;
* versioned derived datasets;
* reproducible figures;
* model evaluation;
* uncertainty workflows.

A future researcher or reviewer should ideally be able to determine not only what result was produced, but how to reproduce it.

The programme should therefore progress from:

> **analysis that works**

toward:

> **analysis that can be inspected, reproduced, challenged, and revised.**

---

# 5. A Multi-Source AMOC Research Framework

No single observational system captures every component, latitude, timescale, or physical expression of the AMOC.

The long-term programme should therefore develop the capability to investigate multiple observing systems and data products without erasing their differences.

Potential sources include:

* RAPID;
* OSNAP;
* MOVE;
* SAMBA;
* Argo;
* hydrographic observations;
* satellite products;
* reanalyses;
* climate-model output;
* reconstructions.

The objective is not to create one enormous table containing everything vaguely associated with Atlantic circulation.

Instead, the programme should build a structured framework in which datasets can be:

* studied independently;
* compared where appropriate;
* integrated where scientifically justified;
* kept separate when definitions or measurement systems are incompatible.

This distinction will become increasingly important as the project grows.

---

# 6. An Evolving Literature and Evidence System

The preliminary reference register created during Phase 0 is only the beginning.

The long-term literature system should evolve into a research database capable of tracking:

* citation metadata;
* evidence type;
* research question;
* dataset used;
* model family;
* methodology;
* geographic coverage;
* temporal coverage;
* principal findings;
* uncertainty;
* limitations;
* conflicting evidence;
* project relevance;
* reading status;
* verification status.

Over time, this system should make it possible to examine not only individual papers but relationships among them.

For example:

* Which datasets appear most frequently in AMOC trend studies?
* Which conclusions depend strongly on particular reconstructions?
* Which findings are supported by independent evidence types?
* Where do model and observational studies disagree?
* Which questions remain limited by observational duration?

The literature system should therefore become an analytical resource in its own right.

---

# 7. A Record of Scientific Uncertainty

One of the programme’s defining long-term characteristics should be its treatment of uncertainty.

The project should not gradually remove uncertainty from its communication as outputs become more sophisticated.

It should become better at describing it.

Over time, uncertainty documentation may include:

* measurement uncertainty;
* sampling uncertainty;
* reconstruction uncertainty;
* methodological uncertainty;
* parameter sensitivity;
* structural model uncertainty;
* ensemble spread;
* natural variability;
* scenario uncertainty;
* interpretation uncertainty.

Different kinds of uncertainty should not be collapsed into one generic warning.

The programme should increasingly distinguish:

> what is uncertain, why it is uncertain, how much that uncertainty matters, and whether it can realistically be reduced.

---

# 8. Replication and Reproduction of Published Research

A significant future direction may involve reproducing or replicating published AMOC analyses.

Possible work could include:

* recreating published figures;
* reproducing statistical analyses;
* implementing published indicators;
* comparing methodological choices;
* testing sensitivity;
* applying methods to updated records;
* examining whether results remain robust under alternative preprocessing.

Replication work would serve several purposes.

It could:

* deepen understanding;
* test reproducibility;
* expose methodological assumptions;
* identify sensitivity;
* connect published literature with open computation.

The objective would not be to approach replication as an attempt to “prove researchers wrong.”

It would be to understand exactly how scientific conclusions are constructed.

---

# 9. Comparative Methodology

As the programme matures, one important contribution may come from systematic comparison of methods.

For example, the same research question may be investigated using:

* alternative trend estimators;
* different detrending approaches;
* multiple filters;
* varying temporal windows;
* different anomaly baselines;
* alternative reconstruction methods;
* different early-warning indicators;
* statistical versus machine-learning approaches.

This could help identify which conclusions are:

* robust;
* method-dependent;
* sensitive to preprocessing;
* dependent on record length;
* dependent on assumptions.

Methodological comparison may therefore become as important as producing individual numerical results.

---

# 10. Physical and Statistical Integration

A central long-term goal is to avoid separating statistical analysis from physical oceanography.

As the project becomes more computationally sophisticated, its scientific standards should become stricter.

Statistical patterns should increasingly be interpreted alongside:

* density structure;
* water masses;
* heat transport;
* freshwater transport;
* atmospheric forcing;
* circulation dynamics;
* spatial structure;
* known physical mechanisms.

The ultimate analytical standard should not be:

> “The model detected a signal.”

It should be:

> “The analysis detected a signal, its methodological sensitivity was evaluated, and its physical interpretation is consistent with or meaningfully challenged by available evidence.”

---

# 11. Advanced Time-Series Research

The programme may eventually develop a specialised AMOC time-series research component.

Potential areas include:

* seasonal decomposition;
* long-term trend estimation;
* autocorrelation;
* spectral structure;
* low-frequency variability;
* structural breaks;
* change points;
* state-space models;
* nonlinear dynamics;
* uncertainty-aware reconstruction.

Longer observational records will naturally create opportunities for analyses that are currently limited by record length.

Methods should therefore be revisited as datasets expand.

An analysis that is inconclusive today may become informative after additional years of observations.

---

# 12. Tipping-Point and Resilience Research

Tipping-point research may become one of the programme’s more advanced components.

Potential future work may investigate:

* theoretical multiple-equilibrium systems;
* hysteresis;
* bifurcation behaviour;
* resilience;
* critical slowing down;
* early-warning indicators;
* noise-induced transitions;
* model-dependent threshold behaviour;
* observational evidence for changing stability.

This subject should remain scientifically separated from sensational claims of imminent collapse.

The long-term objective is not to produce a dramatic date.

It is to understand:

* what tipping means physically;
* which indicators are theoretically expected;
* how reliable those indicators are;
* how they behave in models;
* whether observational records support their use;
* what uncertainty remains.

---

# 13. Machine Learning as a Scientific Tool

Machine learning may become part of later research, but it should remain subordinate to scientific questions.

Potential future uses may include:

* reconstruction;
* anomaly detection;
* clustering;
* regime identification;
* dimensionality reduction;
* pattern classification;
* surrogate modelling;
* nonlinear relationships.

The project should favour comparisons such as:

> simple baseline → statistical method → machine-learning method

rather than assuming that greater computational complexity necessarily represents scientific progress.

Long-term machine-learning work should increasingly consider:

* interpretability;
* physical consistency;
* uncertainty;
* generalisation;
* data leakage;
* overfitting;
* sensitivity;
* reproducibility.

---

# 14. Scientific Visualisation

Visualisation will eventually become a major communication and analytical component.

Potential future outputs include:

* circulation diagrams;
* time-series figures;
* spatial maps;
* transport sections;
* anomaly plots;
* uncertainty bands;
* comparison figures;
* interactive dashboards;
* animated circulation changes.

Scientific visualisation should serve two purposes:

1. reveal structure in the data;
2. communicate that structure accurately.

A visually impressive figure that distorts uncertainty or implies unsupported causality would be a failure regardless of aesthetic quality.

---

# 15. Animation and Dynamic Scientific Communication

The programme may eventually use animation to explain processes that are difficult to communicate through static figures.

Possible topics include:

* AMOC circulation structure;
* water-mass formation;
* density-driven processes;
* seasonal variability;
* observed transport changes;
* relationships among observing systems;
* model scenarios;
* historical development of AMOC research.

Narration may also be incorporated where useful.

Dynamic communication should remain linked to documented scientific sources and analytical outputs.

Animation should clarify complexity rather than dramatise it.

---

# 16. Interactive Research Outputs

As the data infrastructure matures, the programme may develop interactive research tools.

Potential examples include:

* observational dashboards;
* dataset explorers;
* AMOC transport viewers;
* timeline interfaces;
* literature maps;
* methodological comparison tools;
* uncertainty explorers.

Interactive outputs should allow users to examine evidence rather than merely consume conclusions.

Where possible, visual interfaces should link directly to:

* source data;
* methodology;
* definitions;
* uncertainty;
* citations.

# 17. A Public Educational Resource

Over time, the project may become a substantial educational resource for people interested in:

* oceanography;
* climate science;
* AMOC;
* scientific data analysis;
* reproducible research.

The long-term educational archive may contain:

* foundational articles;
* glossaries;
* diagrams;
* mathematical notes;
* code explanations;
* dataset guides;
* methodological tutorials;
* research summaries;
* animations.

The value of these materials should come from their integration with actual research.

Readers should be able to move from:

> “What is the AMOC?”

toward:

> “How is AMOC transport calculated, what evidence supports our understanding, and why are some conclusions uncertain?”

---

# 18. A Science-Communication Platform

The project may eventually develop into a broader science-communication platform surrounding its research.

Different communication channels may serve different purposes:

### GitHub

The complete research record.

### Personal Website

Structured scientific and educational articles.

### Professional Platforms

Milestone updates, accessible explanations, methodological reflections, and research findings.

### Visual Media

Animations, diagrams, and narrated explanations.

The communication layer should always remain downstream of the research.

The project will not generate a conclusion because it would make an engaging post.

Research determines the communication.

Communication does not determine the research.

---

# 19. Research Updates Rather Than Finality

Because the programme is long-term, publications may increasingly use language such as:

* current evidence;
* current interpretation;
* preliminary analysis;
* updated result;
* revised conclusion;
* unresolved question.

This reflects scientific reality.

The programme should avoid creating the impression that every publication is the definitive final answer to a research question.

Some findings may become stronger over time.

Others may weaken.

Some may disappear entirely after methodological revision.

The project infrastructure should preserve this history.

---

# 20. Versioned Scientific Conclusions

Scientific conclusions may eventually be versioned alongside documents and code.

For example, a conclusion could evolve from:

### Version 1

Evidence suggests a possible pattern, but the record is short.

### Version 2

Additional data strengthens the observed pattern.

### Version 3

Methodological sensitivity analysis shows that the result depends strongly on preprocessing.

### Version 4

A revised interpretation becomes necessary.

This type of history would provide a more honest representation of research than silently replacing earlier conclusions.

---

# 21. A Research Archive

Over time, the repository should become an archive of the programme’s development.

It may preserve:

* earlier research documents;
* previous analytical methods;
* obsolete datasets;
* superseded figures;
* revisions;
* decision histories;
* methodological changes;
* failed approaches;
* negative results.

Not every failed analysis needs permanent prominence.

But scientifically meaningful failures should not automatically disappear.

Understanding why a method failed can itself be valuable.

---

# 22. Negative and Inconclusive Results

The programme should allow publication of results that are:

* negative;
* inconclusive;
* statistically weak;
* sensitive to methodology;
* inconsistent with expectations.

A research project that publishes only visually attractive or statistically significant findings creates a distorted record.

Long-term credibility requires preserving scientifically informative results even when they fail to produce a strong narrative.

---

# 23. Research Gap Development

The initial research-gap analysis planned for Phase IX should not be treated as a one-time exercise.

Research gaps should evolve as the project develops.

A question initially classified as unresolved may later become:

* partially resolved;
* testable with new data;
* methodologically inaccessible;
* superseded;
* more complex than originally understood.

The programme should therefore maintain a living research-gap system.

---

# 24. Potential Original Research

The programme may eventually produce analyses that go beyond synthesis and replication.

Potential original contributions could emerge through:

* new combinations of compatible open datasets;
* methodological comparison;
* updated observational analysis;
* uncertainty assessment;
* reproducible implementations;
* novel visualisation;
* sensitivity analysis;
* investigation of underexplored relationships.

Any such work should emerge naturally from identified research gaps.

Originality should not be forced for the purpose of claiming novelty.

A rigorous replication or synthesis can be scientifically more valuable than an artificial “novel” result.

---

# 25. Potential Scientific Publication

If later analyses reach sufficient methodological maturity, some components of the programme may become suitable for:

* technical reports;
* preprints;
* conference submissions;
* research collaborations;
* peer-reviewed manuscripts.

This is a possible development path, not a requirement for the programme to be considered successful.

If formal publication is pursued, repository documentation may provide the reproducibility infrastructure supporting that work.

---

# 26. Collaboration

The programme is independently initiated, but its long-term vision is not isolation.

Potential collaboration may eventually involve:

* oceanographers;
* climate scientists;
* statisticians;
* data scientists;
* scientific programmers;
* research software engineers;
* visualisation specialists;
* educators;
* other independent researchers.

Collaboration could improve:

* domain expertise;
* methodology;
* validation;
* replication;
* peer feedback;
* computational capacity;
* communication.

The programme should remain structurally capable of accepting collaboration without sacrificing provenance or documentation.

---

# 27. External Review

Formal peer review is not automatically available to independent repository work.

However, the programme should increasingly seek opportunities for external scrutiny.

This may occur through:

* open-source review;
* expert feedback;
* research communities;
* issue discussions;
* replication by others;
* scientific collaboration;
* conference feedback;
* eventual formal peer review.

External criticism should be treated as input to the research process.

The objective is not to defend every existing conclusion.

It is to improve the reliability of future conclusions.

---

# 28. Interdisciplinary Expansion

AMOC research intersects with many fields.

Future work may develop stronger connections with:

* cryosphere science;
* atmospheric dynamics;
* marine ecology;
* biogeochemistry;
* carbon-cycle science;
* climate impacts;
* risk science;
* economics;
* infrastructure research.

Such expansion should occur only when the required domain knowledge and evidence are sufficient.

The project should not become superficially interdisciplinary by attaching unrelated datasets to AMOC metrics.

Interdisciplinary research must preserve the methodological standards of each domain involved.

---

# 29. Global Impact Research

Phase XV may eventually develop into a substantial research branch.

Possible themes include:

* regional climate;
* sea level;
* precipitation;
* agriculture;
* fisheries;
* ecosystems;
* carbon cycling;
* weather extremes;
* infrastructure;
* economic exposure;
* social resilience.

Each topic will require a separate evidence chain.

The programme should resist converting broad physical relationships into precise socioeconomic forecasts without suitable data and methodology.

---

# 30. Periodic Reassessment of the Programme

The project itself should periodically become an object of evaluation.

Questions may include:

* Are the research questions still scientifically useful?
* Are documentation systems functioning?
* Are conclusions reproducible?
* Are datasets being used appropriately?
* Are analyses becoming unnecessarily complex?
* Are important uncertainties being neglected?
* Has the scope expanded beyond available expertise?
* Are communication outputs accurately reflecting the research?
* Should parts of the roadmap change?

The programme must remain capable of restructuring itself when necessary.

A roadmap is a guide.

It is not a scientific law.

---

# 31. Technology Will Change

The computational tools available during the programme will evolve.

Programming languages, scientific libraries, data platforms, machine-learning methods, and visualisation technologies may change considerably.

The long-term programme should therefore avoid tying its identity to a particular tool.

Python, SQL, notebooks, dashboards, machine learning, or any future technology are means.

The underlying goals remain:

* scientific understanding;
* evidence quality;
* reproducibility;
* interpretation;
* communication.

Tools should be replaced when better tools become appropriate.

Scientific principles should not be replaced merely because software trends change.

---

# 32. What Should Remain Constant

Although the programme is expected to evolve, several principles should remain stable.

## Evidence Before Narrative

Evidence should determine conclusions.

## Research Before Visualisation

Understanding should precede presentation.

## Physical Meaning Before Statistical Excitement

A numerical pattern must be scientifically interpretable.

## Reproducibility Before Convenience

Important analytical results should be traceable.

## Uncertainty Before False Precision

Unknowns should remain visible.

## Revision Before Defensiveness

Conclusions should change when evidence changes.

## Communication Without Distortion

Accessibility should not require sacrificing scientific meaning.

## No Predetermined Conclusion

The programme should remain capable of discovering that an initial expectation was wrong.

These principles form the continuity of the project even when everything around them changes.

---

# 33. Measures of Long-Term Success

The programme should not judge success solely through:

* GitHub stars;
* article views;
* social-media engagement;
* number of datasets;
* number of models;
* number of dashboards;
* quantity of code;
* volume of publications.

These may reflect reach or activity, but not necessarily research quality.

Long-term success should instead be evaluated through questions such as:

1. Has scientific understanding improved?
2. Are evidence sources traceable?
3. Can analyses be reproduced?
4. Are assumptions documented?
5. Is uncertainty characterised honestly?
6. Are conflicting findings represented fairly?
7. Are methods physically and statistically justified?
8. Can conclusions be revised transparently?
9. Are complex concepts communicated without distortion?
10. Does the programme generate useful questions as well as answers?

---

# 34. What the Programme Should Eventually Demonstrate

At maturity, the AMOC Research Program should demonstrate how a complex scientific problem can be approached through a connected research process involving:

> **scientific learning → literature → evidence → data → computation → physical reasoning → uncertainty → interpretation → communication**

The project should make visible the work that usually disappears between raw data and a final graph.

That includes:

* learning;
* uncertainty;
* methodological choices;
* rejected approaches;
* revisions;
* limitations;
* conflicting evidence.

These are not administrative leftovers.

They are part of the scientific process.

---

# 35. A Resource Beyond the Original Researcher

A mature research programme should remain understandable even when the original author is not present to explain it.

The repository should therefore gradually become self-describing.

A future reader should be able to determine:

* what the project investigates;
* how it developed;
* which sources it relies on;
* where data came from;
* how analyses were conducted;
* what conclusions were reached;
* how certain those conclusions are;
* which conclusions were revised;
* what remains unresolved.

This is one of the strongest reasons for building documentation alongside research rather than after it.

---

# 36. Long-Term Independence

The programme should remain intellectually independent.

This does not mean rejecting institutions, established science, or scientific consensus.

It means conclusions are not selected because they are:

* popular;
* dramatic;
* politically convenient;
* professionally advantageous;
* emotionally satisfying.

The programme should remain free to conclude:

* that evidence is strong;
* that evidence is weak;
* that competing explanations remain plausible;
* that a result cannot yet be established.

Independence is therefore a commitment to the research process rather than distance from the scientific community.

---

# 37. An Expanding but Disciplined Programme

The project may eventually become very large.

That alone would not make it successful.

Growth should remain structured.

New branches should connect back to:

* research objectives;
* scientific relevance;
* evidence requirements;
* scope boundaries;
* methodology;
* reproducibility.

The programme should expand like a research system, not like a folder in which increasingly unrelated interesting things accumulate until no one remembers why they are there.

---

# 38. The Longest-Term Question

The programme begins with AMOC, but its deepest research question is broader:

> **How can complex Earth-system evidence be investigated computationally without losing the physical meaning, uncertainty, and scientific context that make the data interpretable?**

The AMOC provides an unusually demanding environment in which to explore that question.

It involves:

* incomplete observations;
* multiple timescales;
* competing evidence;
* nonlinear dynamics;
* model uncertainty;
* global connections;
* public interest;
* potentially significant consequences.

Building a rigorous research process around such a system may therefore produce lessons that extend beyond AMOC research itself.

---

# 39. Final Vision

The ultimate vision is not a repository containing an impressive quantity of climate data.

It is not a dashboard announcing whether the AMOC is “fine” or “collapsing.”

It is not a machine-learning model producing a dramatic date.

It is a research programme in which someone can move from a scientific question to the evidence behind it, examine the data, inspect the methods, understand the physical reasoning, see the uncertainty, reproduce the analysis, and evaluate the conclusion.

The project should remain useful even when its conclusions change.

Ideally, it should become more useful **because** those changes are documented.

---

## Closing Statement

The AMOC Research Program begins from a simple position:

The Atlantic circulation is important enough to study seriously and complex enough to resist simple answers.

The programme will therefore continue to grow through sustained research rather than through a race toward a final conclusion.

Its long-term purpose is to build a transparent chain connecting:

> **knowledge → evidence → computation → interpretation → communication**

Every major expansion should strengthen that chain.

Every conclusion should remain open to evidence.

Every uncertainty should remain visible until there is a scientific reason for reducing it.

And the programme should always remain capable of asking the question with which it began:

> **What do we currently know, how do we know it, how certain are we, and what remains unknown?**
