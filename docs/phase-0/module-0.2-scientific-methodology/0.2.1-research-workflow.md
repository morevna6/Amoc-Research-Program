# 0.2.1 — Research Workflow

**Phase:** 0 — Research Framework
**Module:** 0.2 — Scientific Methodology
**Status:** Initial publication
**Version:** 0.1.0
**Published:** 16 August 2026
**Author:** Günışığı Aydoğan

> **Publication note:** This document defines the default research workflow of the AMOC Research Program. It is a programme-specific methodological framework rather than a claim that all scientific research follows one universal sequence. Individual research questions may require modification of particular steps, but departures from the workflow should be documented when they materially affect interpretation, reproducibility, or evidential strength.

---

## Research Workflow

The **AMOC Research Program** requires a repeatable process connecting scientific questions to evidence, computation, interpretation, and communication.

The purpose of this workflow is not to make research artificially linear.

Scientific investigation is rarely linear.

Researchers revisit assumptions.

Datasets reveal unexpected limitations.

Methods fail.

New literature changes the question.

Results create better questions than the ones that produced them.

The programme therefore treats research as an **iterative but traceable process**.

The workflow establishes a default sequence while allowing justified movement between stages.

Its central principle is:

> **A conclusion should be traceable backward through interpretation, analysis, methods, data, evidence, and the research question that produced it.**

The programme-wide workflow is:

> **Question → Context → Evidence → Research Design → Data → Validation → Exploration → Analysis → Robustness → Interpretation → Comparison → Reproduction → Communication → Reassessment**

---

# 1. Research Begins With a Question

Research should begin with a defined scientific question rather than with an available dataset, visualisation technique, or modelling tool.

Before starting an investigation, the programme should identify:

* the research question;
* why the question matters;
* what physical process it concerns;
* what evidence would be relevant;
* what type of answer is realistically possible;
* what the project does not yet know.

A useful research question should be sufficiently specific to guide evidence selection and methodology.

For example, the programme should prefer questions such as:

> How does observed AMOC transport variability at a particular observing latitude change across the available instrumental record?

rather than:

> What is happening to the AMOC?

The broader question may motivate research, but the narrower question can be operationalised.

---

## 1.1 Question Record

Each substantial investigation should eventually record:

* **Question ID**
* **Research question**
* **Date opened**
* **Relevant phase and module**
* **Scientific motivation**
* **Required evidence**
* **Potential datasets**
* **Expected methods**
* **Known limitations**
* **Current status**
* **Related questions**
* **Final disposition**

Possible final dispositions include:

* answered;
* partially answered;
* unresolved;
* abandoned;
* superseded;
* reframed.

Abandoned or unresolved questions should not disappear merely because they failed to produce a clean result.

---

# 2. Establish Scientific Context

Before selecting data or methods, the project should determine what is already known about the question.

This stage may include:

* foundational scientific literature;
* major review papers;
* assessment reports;
* observing-programme documentation;
* relevant theoretical work;
* existing datasets;
* previous analyses;
* known scientific disagreements.

The purpose is not yet to perform the complete systematic literature review planned elsewhere in Phase 0.

The purpose is to avoid designing an analysis without understanding the scientific context in which it belongs.

---

## 2.1 Context Questions

Before proceeding, the project should be able to answer:

1. What physical mechanism is involved?
2. How is the relevant quantity defined?
3. How has it previously been measured or estimated?
4. What datasets have been used?
5. What methods have been applied?
6. What major conclusions already exist?
7. Where do studies disagree?
8. What limitations are already known?
9. What would this project add?

If the project cannot yet answer these questions adequately, further background research is required before analysis begins.

---

# 3. Define the Type of Investigation

Not every project question represents the same kind of scientific activity.

Before designing the analysis, the programme should classify the investigation.

Possible categories include:

* foundational learning;
* literature synthesis;
* descriptive analysis;
* exploratory analysis;
* confirmatory analysis;
* replication;
* reproduction;
* methodological comparison;
* hypothesis testing;
* physical calculation;
* reconstruction;
* model evaluation;
* sensitivity analysis;
* prediction;
* impact analysis.

The category matters because different forms of research require different standards of evidence and validation.

For example:

A descriptive analysis does not need to pretend it is hypothesis testing.

An exploratory analysis should not later be presented as if its hypotheses had been specified before seeing the data.

A replication should remain faithful enough to the original methodology for comparison to be meaningful.

---

# 4. Define the Claim Before Choosing the Evidence

The programme should identify what kind of claim the research could potentially support.

Possible claim types include:

* descriptive;
* comparative;
* associational;
* mechanistic;
* causal;
* predictive;
* reconstructive;
* methodological;
* uncertainty-related.

Different claims require different evidence.

For example:

> “Variable A and variable B covary during this observational period”

is not equivalent to:

> “Variable A caused changes in variable B.”

Similarly:

> “A model simulates weakening under a forcing scenario”

is not equivalent to:

> “Observations demonstrate that the same future outcome will occur.”

The proposed claim type should therefore constrain the research design.

---

# 5. Build an Initial Evidence Map

Before committing to a dataset or analytical method, the programme should identify the relevant evidence landscape.

An initial evidence map may include:

* direct observations;
* derived observational products;
* hydrography;
* proxies;
* reconstructions;
* reanalyses;
* theoretical studies;
* process models;
* climate-model simulations;
* projections;
* review literature;
* assessment reports.

The map should identify:

* evidence relevant to the research question;
* evidence that may provide independent support;
* evidence that may conflict;
* evidence that cannot legitimately answer the question.

This prevents the project from selecting only the evidence easiest to obtain or most likely to support an expected conclusion.

---

# 6. Formulate Hypotheses Where Appropriate

Not every investigation requires a formal hypothesis.

Exploratory research, descriptive analysis, methodological development, and literature synthesis may proceed without one.

Where formal hypothesis testing is appropriate, the programme should distinguish clearly among:

* scientific hypothesis;
* statistical hypothesis;
* alternative explanations;
* expected observable implications.

A scientific hypothesis should describe something about the physical system.

A statistical hypothesis describes the expected behaviour of a measurable quantity under a statistical framework.

These are related but not identical.

---

## 6.1 Hypothesis Record

Where applicable, record:

* primary hypothesis;
* null hypothesis;
* alternative hypotheses;
* expected physical mechanism;
* expected observable signal;
* relevant variables;
* potential confounders;
* evidence that would weaken the hypothesis;
* evidence that would remain inconclusive.

The project should avoid hypotheses that can survive every possible outcome.

---

# 7. Separate Exploratory and Confirmatory Work

The programme distinguishes between:

## Exploratory Analysis

Used to:

* understand datasets;
* identify possible relationships;
* generate hypotheses;
* investigate unexpected behaviour;
* evaluate candidate methods.

Exploration is encouraged.

However, results discovered during exploration should be labelled accordingly.

---

## Confirmatory Analysis

Used to evaluate a previously defined question or hypothesis using a method chosen before evaluating the target result.

Where feasible, confirmatory work should specify in advance:

* primary variables;
* analysis period;
* preprocessing;
* statistical method;
* significance or decision criteria;
* sensitivity tests;
* exclusions;
* expected output.

The same data may sometimes support both exploratory and confirmatory work, but the distinction should remain documented.

---

# 8. Design the Research Before Running the Analysis

A research design should connect:

> **Question → Evidence → Variables → Method → Validation → Interpretation**

Before implementation, the project should specify:

* research question;
* study design;
* datasets;
* variables;
* temporal domain;
* spatial domain;
* preprocessing;
* statistical or physical methods;
* assumptions;
* comparison methods;
* validation strategy;
* uncertainty treatment;
* expected outputs.

The design does not need to predict every implementation detail.

Its purpose is to prevent analytical choices from becoming entirely outcome-driven.

---

# 9. Dataset Selection

Datasets should be selected according to scientific suitability rather than convenience.

Selection criteria may include:

* relevance to the research question;
* physical definition;
* observational methodology;
* spatial coverage;
* temporal coverage;
* resolution;
* uncertainty;
* provenance;
* documentation quality;
* version availability;
* citation requirements;
* licence;
* reproducibility of access.

A familiar dataset is not automatically the correct dataset.

A large dataset is not automatically better than a smaller but physically appropriate one.

---

# 10. Dataset Provenance

Every analytical dataset should remain traceable to its source.

At minimum, record where available:

* provider;
* dataset name;
* version;
* DOI or persistent identifier;
* source URL;
* access date;
* licence;
* citation;
* variables used;
* temporal coverage;
* spatial coverage;
* original format.

If the source changes over time, version information becomes especially important.

---

# 11. Preserve Raw Data

Raw source data should remain unchanged wherever technically possible.

The programme should distinguish among:

* raw data;
* interim data;
* cleaned data;
* processed data;
* derived data;
* analytical outputs.

Transformations should occur in reproducible stages.

A processed file should never silently replace the raw source.

---

# 12. Validate Before Analysing

Data validation should occur before scientific interpretation.

Validation may include:

* schema inspection;
* variable names;
* units;
* dimensions;
* coordinate systems;
* timestamp format;
* temporal ordering;
* geographic coverage;
* missing values;
* duplicate records;
* invalid values;
* physically implausible values;
* version consistency;
* metadata consistency.

Scientific datasets may also require domain-specific checks.

For example, an unusual value should not automatically be removed as an outlier merely because it appears statistically extreme.

It may represent:

* a real physical event;
* an instrument problem;
* a processing artefact;
* a unit mismatch;
* a valid extreme observation.

Its meaning must be investigated.

---

# 13. Document Data Transformations

Every meaningful transformation should eventually be reproducible.

Examples include:

* filtering;
* interpolation;
* resampling;
* detrending;
* smoothing;
* aggregation;
* anomaly calculation;
* baseline selection;
* unit conversion;
* coordinate transformation;
* variable derivation;
* missing-data treatment.

For each major transformation, record:

1. what was changed;
2. why it was changed;
3. how it was changed;
4. which parameters were used;
5. how the choice may affect interpretation.

---

# 14. Conduct Exploratory Data Analysis

Exploratory data analysis should be used to understand the dataset before formal modelling.

Potential tasks include:

* coverage inspection;
* missingness analysis;
* descriptive statistics;
* distributions;
* temporal behaviour;
* spatial structure;
* seasonal structure;
* anomalies;
* relationships among variables;
* unexpected values;
* discontinuities.

Exploration should be generous.

Interpretation should be cautious.

An exploratory graph can generate a research question.

It should not automatically answer one.

---

# 15. Establish Baseline Methods

Before using complex methods, the project should identify reasonable baselines.

Depending on the question, a baseline might include:

* mean or climatological reference;
* simple linear trend;
* persistence model;
* seasonal model;
* conventional regression;
* simple statistical reconstruction;
* physically established calculation.

Complex methods should be compared against simpler alternatives where meaningful.

If an elaborate model cannot outperform or clarify a sensible baseline, its scientific value should be questioned.

---

# 16. Conduct Formal Analysis

Formal analysis should follow the research design and use methods appropriate to:

* the research question;
* the data-generating process;
* temporal structure;
* spatial structure;
* uncertainty;
* available sample size;
* known limitations.

Possible later methods may include:

* physical calculations;
* regression;
* trend estimation;
* decomposition;
* spectral analysis;
* autocorrelation;
* change-point detection;
* early-warning indicators;
* numerical models;
* machine learning.

Methods should not be selected solely because they are available in a software library.

---

# 17. Quantify Uncertainty

Uncertainty should be addressed during analysis rather than added after the primary result is known.

Depending on the problem, this may involve:

* confidence intervals;
* credible intervals;
* measurement-error propagation;
* bootstrap estimates;
* ensemble spread;
* sensitivity ranges;
* alternative model specifications;
* parameter uncertainty;
* observational uncertainty.

Where uncertainty cannot be quantified adequately, it should still be described explicitly.

---

# 18. Perform Sensitivity and Robustness Analysis

A scientific result should not be considered robust merely because one implementation produced it.

Where relevant, test sensitivity to choices such as:

* analysis period;
* baseline period;
* detrending method;
* smoothing method;
* filtering;
* temporal resolution;
* spatial domain;
* window size;
* missing-data treatment;
* parameter selection;
* model specification;
* exclusion criteria.

The purpose is to determine whether the conclusion is:

* robust;
* moderately sensitive;
* highly method-dependent;
* unstable.

Method dependence is itself a result.

---

# 19. Investigate Alternative Explanations

Before accepting the preferred interpretation, the programme should ask:

> **What else could produce this result?**

Possible alternatives may include:

* natural variability;
* common forcing;
* observational artefacts;
* methodological artefacts;
* sampling effects;
* autocorrelation;
* structural breaks;
* confounding variables;
* alternative physical mechanisms.

The strongest interpretation is not necessarily the most interesting one.

It is the interpretation that survives the strongest reasonable alternatives.

---

# 20. Connect Results to Physical Meaning

Statistical output must eventually return to the physical system.

Interpretation should examine:

* the physical quantity involved;
* known mechanisms;
* relevant timescales;
* spatial relationships;
* energy or mass constraints where relevant;
* consistency with established oceanography;
* consistency with observational methodology.

A pattern without plausible physical interpretation should remain provisional until its meaning is understood.

---

# 21. Compare With Existing Scientific Literature

Project-generated findings should be compared with existing research.

The comparison should ask:

* Is the result consistent with previous studies?
* If not, why might it differ?
* Are different datasets involved?
* Are different periods involved?
* Are definitions different?
* Are methods different?
* Is the disagreement within uncertainty?
* Does the project reveal a genuine conflict or merely a methodological difference?

Agreement with existing literature does not automatically prove the result correct.

Disagreement does not automatically mean the project discovered an error in established science.

Both require investigation.

---

# 22. Distinguish Result From Interpretation

The programme should separate:

### Result

What the analysis actually produced.

### Interpretation

What the result may mean scientifically.

For example:

> “Lag-1 autocorrelation increased during the analysed interval”

is a result.

> “The system is approaching a tipping point”

is an interpretation requiring substantially more evidence.

Keeping these layers separate reduces the risk of converting statistical behaviour into unsupported physical claims.

---

# 23. Apply Claim Strength Proportionally

The language of the conclusion should reflect the strength of the evidence.

Possible formulations include:

* observed;
* calculated;
* consistent with;
* suggests;
* supports;
* provides limited evidence for;
* cannot distinguish between;
* remains uncertain;
* does not support;
* requires further investigation.

Strong language should require strong evidence.

Precision of wording is part of methodology, not merely writing style.

---

# 24. Reproduce the Analysis

Before publication of an important result, the programme should attempt to reproduce it from the documented workflow.

A reproducibility check may include:

1. clean environment;
2. documented dependencies;
3. source data retrieval;
4. preprocessing execution;
5. analysis execution;
6. figure regeneration;
7. output comparison.

A result that only exists inside one interactive notebook state is not yet a mature research output.

---

# 25. Preserve Failed and Negative Results Where Scientifically Useful

Research does not always produce a positive finding.

The programme may retain records of analyses that:

* failed;
* contradicted expectations;
* produced no significant result;
* were too sensitive to methodology;
* could not answer the question;
* revealed inadequate data.

Such outcomes may prevent future repetition of the same mistake and help clarify the limits of available evidence.

---

# 26. Internal Review Before Publication

Before a major project output is published, review:

## Scientific

* Is the physical interpretation justified?
* Are alternative explanations considered?
* Are claims proportional to evidence?

## Methodological

* Is the method appropriate?
* Are assumptions documented?
* Has sensitivity been considered?

## Data

* Is provenance clear?
* Were transformations documented?
* Are known quality problems reported?

## Reproducibility

* Can the analysis be rerun?
* Are dependencies and parameters known?

## Communication

* Are observation and inference distinguished?
* Is uncertainty visible?
* Does the title overstate the result?

---

# 27. Publish With Provenance

A formal project output should link, where applicable, to:

* research question;
* literature;
* datasets;
* methodology;
* code;
* assumptions;
* limitations;
* figures;
* related analyses;
* revision history.

The ideal publication chain is:

> **claim → interpretation → result → analysis → processed data → source data → methodology → research question**

---

# 28. Communicate at the Appropriate Layer

The same research may be communicated through different formats.

Possible layers include:

* full research documentation;
* technical explanation;
* educational material;
* public communication.

Simplification is permitted.

Scientific distortion is not.

A public-facing summary should never contain stronger claims than the technical research supporting it.

---

# 29. Record Decisions

Important methodological decisions should eventually be recorded in a project decision log.

Examples include:

* why one dataset was selected over another;
* why a time period was chosen;
* why a filtering method was used;
* why a model was rejected;
* why a research question was reframed;
* why an interpretation was revised.

This prevents later results from becoming detached from the reasoning that created them.

---

# 30. Record Assumptions and Limitations

Each substantial analysis should identify its important assumptions.

These may concern:

* measurement;
* stationarity;
* independence;
* missing data;
* spatial representativeness;
* model structure;
* physical interpretation.

Limitations should be recorded alongside results rather than discovered only during criticism.

---

# 31. Reassess After Publication

Publication does not terminate the research process.

A published conclusion should be reconsidered when:

* new data become available;
* a dataset is revised;
* methodology improves;
* errors are discovered;
* new literature emerges;
* external criticism identifies weaknesses;
* additional observations alter the interpretation.

The workflow therefore ends with reassessment rather than finality.

---

# 32. Correction and Revision

If an error materially affects a published result, the programme should:

1. identify the error;
2. preserve the original version where practical;
3. correct the analysis;
4. explain what changed;
5. determine whether interpretation changes;
6. update affected documents;
7. record the revision in the changelog.

Corrections should be visible.

Silent replacement weakens scientific traceability.

---

# 33. Research Workflow States

A research item may move through the following states:

| State               | Meaning                                        |
| ------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- |
| Proposed            | Question identified but not yet evaluated      |
| Background Research | Scientific context being established           |
| Designed            | Evidence and methodology defined               |
| Data Acquisition    | Required evidence being obtained               |
| Validation          | Data and metadata being checked                |
| Exploratory         | Patterns and data behaviour being investigated |
| Analytical          | Formal analysis underway                       |
| Robustness Review   | Sensitivity and alternatives being tested      |
| Interpretation      | Scientific meaning being evaluated             |
| Reproduction Check  | Workflow being rerun and verified              |
| Draft               | Results being documented                       |
| Published           | Research output publicly available             |
| Revised             | Published interpretation updated               |
| Superseded          | Replaced by a later analysis                   |
| Unresolved          | Current evidence insufficient                  |
| Closed              | Research question concluded for current scope  |

These states may later become part of the project’s research database or tracking system.

---

# 34. Minimum Standard for a Project-Generated Scientific Finding

Before an analytical finding is treated as a formal project result, the programme should normally be able to identify:

### Question

What scientific question was investigated?

### Evidence

What data or literature support the analysis?

### Provenance

Where did the evidence come from?

### Method

How was the result produced?

### Assumptions

What assumptions were required?

### Validation

How was data quality or model validity assessed?

### Uncertainty

What uncertainty remains?

### Sensitivity

Does the result depend strongly on analytical choices?

### Alternatives

What other explanations were considered?

### Physical Meaning

Is the interpretation physically plausible?

### Comparison

How does the result compare with published research?

### Reproducibility

Can the result be regenerated?

### Boundaries

What does the result not establish?

If several of these questions cannot yet be answered, the result should remain exploratory.

---

# 35. Workflow Exceptions

Some investigations will not require every stage.

For example:

A literature-history document may not require data acquisition.

A mathematical derivation may not require exploratory data analysis.

A dataset-validation study may not require prediction.

A replication may inherit part of its research design from an existing paper.

The workflow should therefore not become ritualistic.

Steps may be marked:

* applicable;
* not applicable;
* deferred;
* modified.

However, the reason for materially changing the workflow should be documented.

---

# 36. Workflow Iteration

Research may return to earlier stages.

For example:

> Analysis reveals poor data quality
> → return to dataset selection.

> Literature reveals a stronger alternative explanation
> → return to research design.

> Sensitivity analysis invalidates the preferred method
> → return to methodology selection.

> New observations change the conclusion
> → return to analysis and interpretation.

Iteration is expected.

Loss of traceability is not.

---

# 37. Programme-Wide Research Sequence

The complete default workflow is:

1. **Define the research question**
2. **Establish scientific context**
3. **Classify the investigation**
4. **Define the potential claim**
5. **Map relevant evidence**
6. **Formulate hypotheses where appropriate**
7. **Distinguish exploratory and confirmatory work**
8. **Design the research**
9. **Select datasets**
10. **Record provenance**
11. **Preserve raw data**
12. **Validate data**
13. **Document transformations**
14. **Conduct exploratory analysis**
15. **Establish baselines**
16. **Conduct formal analysis**
17. **Quantify uncertainty**
18. **Test robustness and sensitivity**
19. **Investigate alternative explanations**
20. **Connect results to physical mechanisms**
21. **Compare with published literature**
22. **Separate result from interpretation**
23. **Calibrate claim strength**
24. **Reproduce the analysis**
25. **Preserve informative negative results**
26. **Conduct internal review**
27. **Publish with provenance**
28. **Communicate at the appropriate level**
29. **Record decisions**
30. **Record assumptions and limitations**
31. **Reassess when evidence changes**
32. **Correct and revise transparently**

This sequence is intentionally more demanding than:

> download → clean → visualise → model → publish

because scientific research requires more than successful execution of code.

## Closing Statement

The AMOC Research Program does not treat research as the act of applying increasingly complex techniques to available data.

Research begins with a question.

It proceeds through evidence.

It is constrained by methodology.

It is tested through uncertainty and alternatives.

It becomes meaningful through physical interpretation.

It becomes credible through reproducibility.

And it remains scientific only while conclusions remain open to revision.

The purpose of this workflow is therefore not to prevent research from changing direction.

It is to make every important change of direction visible.
