# 0.2.2 — Evidence Hierarchy and Evaluation

**Phase:** 0 — Research Framework
**Module:** 0.2 — Scientific Methodology
**Status:** Initial publication
**Version:** 0.1.0
**Published:** 16 August 2026
**Author:** F. Günışığı (Morevna) Aydoğan

> **Publication note:** This document defines how the AMOC Research Program will classify, evaluate, compare, and combine scientific evidence. The framework is project-specific and is not intended to represent a universal hierarchy applicable to every scientific discipline. Evidence strength will be evaluated relative to the claim being investigated.

---

## Evidence Hierarchy and Evaluation

Scientific research requires more than collecting sources.

A large bibliography can still support a weak conclusion if:

* the sources address different questions;
* measurements are indirect;
* datasets are not independent;
* methodological weaknesses are ignored;
* uncertainty is poorly characterised;
* contradictory evidence is excluded;
* model results are treated as observations;
* secondary summaries replace primary evidence;
* multiple papers depend on the same underlying dataset and are mistakenly counted as independent confirmation.

The AMOC Research Program therefore distinguishes between:

> **having evidence**

and:

> **having evidence capable of supporting a particular claim.**

The programme will not use a universal rule in which one evidence category is always superior to every other category.

Instead, evidence will be evaluated according to:

> **relevance → directness → quality → independence → uncertainty → consistency → applicability → convergence**

A form of evidence may be highly valuable for one question and inappropriate for another.

---

# 1. Evidence Is Evaluated Relative to a Claim

Evidence cannot be graded meaningfully without first identifying what it is supposed to support.

For example:

A direct observational array may provide strong evidence concerning AMOC transport at a particular latitude during its observational period.

The same observational record may provide limited evidence concerning AMOC behaviour several centuries before measurements began.

A palaeoclimate reconstruction may provide information about much longer timescales.

However, it cannot be treated as equivalent to a modern direct transport measurement.

A climate model may be necessary for investigating future forcing scenarios or counterfactual experiments.

It should not be presented as a direct observation of future reality.

Therefore, the first evidence-evaluation question is:

> **Evidence for what claim?**

---

# 2. No Universal Evidence Ladder

The programme does not adopt a simple hierarchy such as:

> observation > reconstruction > model > theory

for every scientific question.

Such a structure would be misleading.

Different evidence types answer different questions.

For example:

### Direct observations

May be strongest for:

* measured contemporary variability;
* instrument-period transport estimates;
* observed physical conditions.

### Reconstructions

May be necessary for:

* periods before direct monitoring;
* long-term historical variability;
* palaeoclimate investigation.

### Process and theoretical models

May be particularly valuable for:

* mechanisms;
* stability;
* feedbacks;
* causal hypotheses;
* idealised behaviour.

### Climate models

May be necessary for:

* controlled forcing experiments;
* future scenarios;
* ensemble behaviour;
* counterfactual comparison.

### Scientific assessments

May be strongest for:

* synthesising large bodies of literature;
* evaluating agreement;
* expressing collective confidence;
* identifying major uncertainties.

The correct evidence depends on the question.

---

# 3. Evidence Classes

The programme will classify scientific material by evidence type before evaluating its strength.

The principal classes are:

1. **Direct observational evidence**
2. **Derived observational evidence**
3. **Hydrographic evidence**
4. **Proxy and palaeoclimate evidence**
5. **Reconstruction evidence**
6. **Reanalysis products**
7. **Theoretical evidence**
8. **Process-model evidence**
9. **Climate-model simulations**
10. **Model projections**
11. **Statistical evidence**
12. **Methodological evidence**
13. **Review literature**
14. **Scientific assessments**
15. **Institutional documentation**
16. **Public or communication material**

These categories describe what a source represents.

They do not automatically determine its quality.

---

# 4. Direct Observational Evidence

Direct observational evidence includes measurements obtained through scientific observing systems and instrumentation.

Relevant examples may include measurements from:

* moorings;
* CTD instruments;
* ADCP systems;
* Argo floats;
* hydrographic cruises;
* satellite instruments;
* current meters;
* temperature and salinity sensors.

Direct observations are particularly valuable because they maintain a close relationship with physically measured quantities.

However, they still contain limitations.

These may include:

* instrument uncertainty;
* calibration uncertainty;
* limited spatial coverage;
* limited temporal coverage;
* sampling frequency;
* missing observations;
* methodological changes;
* representativeness.

The word **observed** should therefore never be treated as equivalent to **perfectly known**.

---

# 5. Derived Observational Evidence

Many scientifically important quantities are not measured directly.

They are calculated from observations.

AMOC transport estimates, for example, may depend on combinations of:

* directly measured variables;
* inferred transports;
* density structure;
* reference assumptions;
* spatial integration;
* methodological calculations.

The programme will distinguish between:

> **measured variables**

and:

> **derived quantities constructed from measurements.**

Derived observational products remain observationally grounded, but their interpretation also depends on the methodology used to construct them.

---

# 6. Hydrographic Evidence

Hydrographic observations may provide information about:

* temperature;
* salinity;
* density;
* water masses;
* ocean sections;
* circulation structure.

These observations can provide highly valuable physical information.

However, repeated hydrographic sections may represent snapshots separated by substantial time intervals.

Care is therefore required when converting spatial hydrographic evidence into claims about continuous temporal behaviour.

---

# 7. Proxy and Palaeoclimate Evidence

Proxy evidence may allow investigation of periods long before modern instrumental observations.

Potential proxies may reflect aspects of:

* circulation;
* water-mass properties;
* temperature;
* salinity;
* sediment transport;
* chemical composition.

Proxy evidence can extend temporal understanding dramatically.

However, it may also introduce uncertainty concerning:

* proxy calibration;
* dating;
* physical interpretation;
* spatial representativeness;
* temporal resolution;
* preservation;
* reconstruction methodology.

A proxy for an AMOC-related process is not automatically a direct measurement of AMOC strength.

---

# 8. Reconstruction Evidence

Reconstructions attempt to estimate variables or system behaviour that cannot be observed directly across the entire desired period or domain.

They may combine:

* observations;
* proxies;
* statistical models;
* physical relationships;
* reanalysis products;
* model information.

Reconstructions can provide valuable extended records.

Their strength depends on:

* input data;
* reconstruction assumptions;
* validation;
* temporal stability;
* uncertainty;
* methodological sensitivity.

Different reconstructions of the same phenomenon may disagree.

That disagreement must be investigated rather than averaged away automatically.

---

# 9. Reanalysis Evidence

Reanalysis products combine observations with numerical models and data-assimilation systems.

They provide spatially and temporally structured estimates of the physical system.

They are extremely useful for many research questions.

However, they are neither:

* pure observations;

nor:

* unconstrained model simulations.

The programme will treat reanalysis as its own evidence class.

Relevant limitations may include:

* model structure;
* assimilation method;
* changing observational coverage;
* temporal inhomogeneity;
* parameterisation;
* resolution;
* product-specific biases.

Two reanalysis products should not automatically be considered independent evidence if they share substantial observational or methodological foundations.

---

# 10. Theoretical Evidence

Physical and mathematical theory provides evidence concerning what mechanisms are:

* possible;
* expected;
* internally consistent;
* dynamically plausible.

Theoretical work may be particularly important for understanding:

* feedbacks;
* stability;
* multiple equilibria;
* bifurcations;
* conservation relationships;
* transport mechanisms.

Theory may strongly support a mechanism without establishing that the mechanism currently dominates the real-world system.

The programme will therefore distinguish:

> **physically possible**

from:

> **empirically demonstrated.**

---

# 11. Process-Model Evidence

Idealised or process models simplify complex systems to investigate particular mechanisms.

Their strengths may include:

* controlled experimentation;
* mechanistic clarity;
* sensitivity analysis;
* theoretical testing.

Their limitations may include:

* simplified geometry;
* reduced forcing complexity;
* idealised boundary conditions;
* omitted processes;
* limited direct comparability with observations.

A process model may provide powerful evidence about **how something could happen** without proving **that it is currently happening in the real ocean**.

---

# 12. Climate-Model Simulations

Coupled climate models provide representations of interacting components of the Earth system.

They may be used to investigate:

* historical behaviour;
* forcing responses;
* circulation dynamics;
* internal variability;
* future scenarios;
* ensemble behaviour.

Model simulations will be evaluated according to:

* model family;
* resolution;
* forcing;
* parameterisation;
* ensemble size;
* experiment design;
* known biases;
* comparison with observations.

Multiple models may strengthen evidence when they provide genuinely informative independent or structurally diverse lines of analysis.

However, the programme will not equate:

> **many model runs**

with:

> **many independent observations.**

---

# 13. Model Projections

A projection is conditional.

Its interpretation depends on:

* model structure;
* initial conditions where relevant;
* forcing scenario;
* emissions or concentration pathway;
* ensemble design;
* assumptions.

The project will maintain a clear distinction among:

* prediction;
* projection;
* scenario;
* simulation.

A projection answers something closer to:

> **What does the model indicate under these conditions?**

rather than:

> **What will definitely happen?**

---

# 14. Statistical Evidence

Statistical evidence may support conclusions concerning:

* trends;
* relationships;
* distributions;
* change points;
* variability;
* temporal structure;
* anomalies;
* uncertainty.

Its quality depends on:

* sample size;
* data quality;
* statistical assumptions;
* autocorrelation;
* stationarity;
* model specification;
* multiple testing;
* preprocessing;
* uncertainty.

Statistical evidence is not automatically evidence of physical mechanism.

The programme will distinguish:

> **statistical detection**

from:

> **physical explanation.**

---

# 15. Methodological Evidence

Some research primarily evaluates methods rather than the physical system directly.

Methodological evidence may concern:

* estimator performance;
* reconstruction accuracy;
* sensitivity;
* bias;
* preprocessing;
* uncertainty quantification;
* algorithm comparison.

Such studies may substantially affect how physical evidence should be interpreted.

A method shown to be highly sensitive or biased may reduce confidence in conclusions that depend strongly on that method.

---

# 16. Peer-Reviewed Primary Literature

Peer-reviewed research papers are a central evidence source.

However:

> **peer reviewed does not mean unquestionable.**

A paper should still be evaluated for:

* data quality;
* methodology;
* assumptions;
* reproducibility;
* uncertainty;
* applicability;
* consistency with other evidence;
* limitations.

Peer review is an important quality-control mechanism.

It is not a guarantee that a conclusion is final or correct.

---

# 17. Review Articles

Review papers can provide:

* field-level synthesis;
* historical context;
* terminology;
* identification of major debates;
* summaries of evidence;
* extensive bibliographies.

They are particularly useful for navigating unfamiliar scientific areas.

However, where a specific quantitative or methodological claim matters, the programme should seek the relevant primary source when possible.

A review should not automatically replace the research it summarises.

---

# 18. Scientific Assessments

Large scientific assessments can provide high-value synthesis of extensive bodies of evidence.

They may be particularly useful for:

* identifying areas of agreement;
* identifying disagreement;
* evaluating confidence;
* integrating multiple evidence types;
* communicating uncertainty.

The IPCC assessment framework, for example, evaluates findings through underlying evidence and agreement and uses calibrated language to communicate confidence and likelihood where appropriate.

The programme may use such assessments as major synthesis sources.

However, assessment reports should not eliminate the need to inspect primary literature when a research question requires detailed methodological evaluation.

---

# 19. Institutional Documentation

Institutional documentation may include material produced by:

* observing programmes;
* universities;
* research laboratories;
* national science agencies;
* data centres.

This material can be particularly important for:

* dataset definitions;
* instrumentation;
* processing methodology;
* release information;
* technical documentation;
* metadata;
* programme history.

For understanding how an observing system constructs a published variable, official technical documentation may be more useful than a general scientific review.

---

# 20. Public Communication Sources

Sources such as:

* news articles;
* press releases;
* science magazines;
* institutional news pages;
* educational websites;
* social media;

may help identify:

* new publications;
* emerging debates;
* terminology;
* public interpretation.

They should generally **not serve as the final evidence source for a scientific claim** when the underlying scientific source is accessible.

The programme should trace claims backward whenever possible:

> **communication source → institutional source → paper → dataset or method**

Public communication is useful for discovery.

It is rarely the end of scientific verification.

---

# 21. Evidence Evaluation Dimensions

Each important source should eventually be evaluated across several dimensions.

## 21.1 Relevance

Does the evidence actually address the research question?

## 21.2 Directness

How directly does the evidence represent the quantity or mechanism being discussed?

## 21.3 Methodological Quality

Are the methods appropriate, documented, and scientifically defensible?

## 21.4 Data Quality

Are the underlying observations or inputs suitable?

## 21.5 Independence

Does the evidence represent a genuinely distinct line of investigation?

## 21.6 Uncertainty

Are important uncertainties quantified or discussed?

## 21.7 Reproducibility

Can the methodology and analysis be inspected or reproduced?

## 21.8 Consistency

Is the finding consistent with other relevant evidence?

## 21.9 Applicability

Does the result apply to the system, region, timescale, or question being investigated?

## 21.10 Transparency

Are assumptions and limitations visible?

---

# 22. Relevance Before Prestige

A prestigious publication cannot compensate for irrelevant evidence.

A high-profile paper addressing one AMOC metric does not automatically provide evidence for every question concerning Atlantic circulation.

The programme should therefore ask:

> **Does this evidence actually answer the question?**

before asking:

> **Where was it published?**

Journal prestige may provide context.

It is not itself a scientific argument.

---

# 23. Directness of Evidence

Evidence may vary in how directly it represents the target quantity.

For a particular claim, evidence may be classified broadly as:

### Direct

Closely measures or evaluates the target phenomenon.

### Partially Direct

Represents an important component or closely related variable.

### Indirect

Provides information through a proxy, reconstruction, model relationship, or inferred mechanism.

### Contextual

Helps interpret the claim but does not independently test it.

Indirect evidence is not necessarily weak.

It simply requires a longer inferential chain.

That chain should remain visible.

---

# 24. Evidence Independence

Several studies do not necessarily represent several independent pieces of evidence.

Studies may share:

* the same dataset;
* the same reconstruction;
* the same climate-model ensemble;
* the same preprocessing;
* overlapping authorship;
* related algorithms;
* common assumptions.

For example:

> five papers analysing the same observational record

do not automatically equal:

> five independent observational confirmations.

The programme will track shared evidence foundations wherever possible.

---

# 25. Evidence Convergence

Confidence may increase when multiple independent forms of evidence point toward compatible conclusions.

Examples could include convergence among:

* observations;
* physical theory;
* reconstructions;
* models;
* independent datasets.

This is particularly valuable when evidence types have different weaknesses.

A conclusion supported by several genuinely independent approaches may be more robust than one supported repeatedly through the same method and source data.

---

# 26. Agreement Does Not Automatically Equal Truth

High agreement among studies may result from genuine scientific convergence.

But agreement can also arise because studies share:

* datasets;
* assumptions;
* models;
* calibration methods;
* theoretical frameworks.

The programme will therefore distinguish:

> **agreement**

from:

> **independent convergence.**

These are related but not identical.

---

# 27. Disagreement Is Evidence

Scientific disagreement should not be treated as an inconvenience to remove.

Disagreement may reveal:

* data sensitivity;
* methodological differences;
* uncertainty;
* alternative mechanisms;
* structural model differences;
* different spatial or temporal definitions.

When studies conflict, the programme should investigate **why**.

The existence of disagreement may itself provide important information about the maturity of a scientific conclusion.

---

# 28. Conflicting Evidence Protocol

When substantial evidence conflicts, the programme should:

1. identify the precise claims being compared;
2. determine whether the studies actually address the same quantity;
3. compare definitions;
4. compare datasets;
5. compare spatial coverage;
6. compare temporal coverage;
7. compare preprocessing;
8. compare statistical methods;
9. compare model assumptions;
10. compare uncertainty treatment;
11. identify shared dependencies;
12. assess whether differences fall within uncertainty;
13. document unresolved disagreement.

The project should not select a winner merely because one conclusion is more dramatic, convenient, or personally convincing.

---

# 29. Evidence Strength Is Claim-Specific

The programme may classify support for a **specific claim** using qualitative categories such as:

### Strong Support

Multiple high-quality and appropriately direct lines of evidence support the claim, with important alternatives substantially constrained.

### Moderate Support

Relevant evidence supports the claim, but important uncertainty, limited independence, or plausible alternatives remain.

### Limited Support

Some evidence is consistent with the claim, but directness, quality, quantity, independence, or agreement is insufficient for a stronger conclusion.

### Inconclusive

Available evidence does not currently distinguish among major competing interpretations.

### Conflicting

Substantial credible evidence supports incompatible interpretations that cannot yet be reconciled.

### Unsupported

Available evidence does not provide a defensible basis for the claim.

These classifications apply to **claims**, not permanently to papers or datasets.

---

# 30. Evidence Quantity Does Not Equal Evidence Strength

Ten weakly relevant studies do not automatically outweigh two highly relevant and methodologically strong studies.

Evidence evaluation should therefore avoid simple counting.

The programme will consider:

* quality;
* directness;
* independence;
* uncertainty;
* applicability;

alongside quantity.

---

# 31. Source Age and Scientific Currency

Older evidence is not automatically inferior.

Foundational theoretical work may remain highly relevant for decades.

Likewise, newer evidence is not automatically superior simply because it is recent.

Recency becomes especially important when:

* datasets have been extended;
* methods have changed;
* model generations have advanced;
* observational systems have been recalibrated;
* earlier interpretations have been challenged.

The programme should distinguish:

> **historically important**

from:

> **currently authoritative for a specific claim.**

---

# 32. Source Versioning

Where scientific resources are versioned, the programme should record the version used.

This applies to:

* datasets;
* reanalysis products;
* climate-model archives;
* documentation;
* software;
* assessment reports;
* living methodological resources.

A later version may contain:

* additional observations;
* corrected errors;
* revised processing;
* changed metadata.

Evidence should therefore remain linked to the version actually evaluated.

---

# 33. Primary Sources and Secondary Sources

The programme generally prefers primary sources when evaluating:

* original measurements;
* methods;
* numerical results;
* datasets;
* model experiments;
* novel scientific claims.

Secondary sources remain valuable for:

* orientation;
* synthesis;
* identifying literature;
* understanding broad scientific context.

The appropriate chain is often:

> **secondary synthesis → primary study → data/method documentation**

rather than stopping permanently at the synthesis layer.

---

# 34. Evidence and Uncertainty

The strength of evidence cannot be evaluated independently of uncertainty.

Uncertainty may arise from:

* measurement;
* sampling;
* reconstruction;
* methodology;
* statistical estimation;
* model structure;
* parameters;
* forcing;
* natural variability.

The programme should therefore evaluate:

> **What does the evidence show?**

together with:

> **How uncertain is that evidence?**

---

# 35. Confidence Is Not the Same as Probability

The programme will distinguish between:

### Confidence

The degree of confidence in a scientific conclusion based on factors such as evidence, agreement, understanding, and uncertainty.

### Probability or Likelihood

A quantitative or probabilistic statement concerning the occurrence or value of an event or outcome.

These concepts should not be used interchangeably.

Where calibrated confidence language from major scientific assessments is reported, the original terminology should be preserved.

---

For major research questions, the programme may eventually maintain an evidence matrix.

Example structure:

| E-002       | Example claim | Reconstruction | Indirect   | Moderate     | Reconstruction method        | Partial   | Pending            |
| E-003       | Example claim | Model ensemble | Indirect   | Moderate     | Structural model uncertainty | Partial   | Pending            |

The final structure will be implemented during the literature-management and research-database stages.

---

# 37. Evidence Record

Each important source may eventually receive a structured evidence record containing:

* Evidence ID
* Citation
* Evidence class
* Research question
* Claim supported
* Claim challenged
* Dataset
* Model
* Temporal coverage
* Geographic coverage
* Method
* Directness
* Independence
* Main assumptions
* Main uncertainty
* Key limitations
* Relationship to other evidence
* Verification status
* Project evaluation
* Notes

This converts literature management from bibliography storage into scientific evidence management.

---

# 38. Verification Status

Sources may later receive verification states such as:

### Identified

Source discovered but not yet reviewed.

### Retrieved

Full source obtained.

### Reviewed

Relevant content examined.

### Method Checked

Methodology specifically reviewed.

### Data Checked

Underlying dataset or source identified.

### Verified for Claim

Source confirmed to support the exact project statement associated with it.

### Superseded

Newer or corrected evidence replaces the source for a particular use.

A paper appearing in the reference database does not automatically mean every claim attributed to it has been verified.

---

# 39. Citation Is Not Evidence Evaluation

Citing a paper demonstrates where a statement came from.

It does not demonstrate that:

* the paper is methodologically sound;
* the evidence is relevant;
* uncertainty is acceptable;
* competing evidence does not exist;
* the project interpreted the paper correctly.

Citation and evaluation are separate tasks.

Both are required.

---

# 40. Evidence Selection and Confirmation Bias

Researchers can unintentionally select evidence that supports expectations.

The programme should reduce this risk by:

* searching for contradictory literature;
* recording competing interpretations;
* documenting search criteria;
* distinguishing discovery from evaluation;
* preserving negative findings;
* using structured evidence tables;
* revisiting assumptions.

The question should not be:

> **Which sources support my interpretation?**

It should be:

> **What does the relevant evidence landscape contain?**

---

# 41. Evidence Should Challenge the Project

A healthy research framework should permit evidence to weaken or overturn previous project assumptions.

The programme should therefore actively identify evidence that could:

* contradict a hypothesis;
* weaken a preferred interpretation;
* expose methodological dependence;
* reveal incompatible datasets;
* demonstrate greater uncertainty than expected.

Evidence that changes the project’s mind is not an obstacle.

It is one of the main reasons the project exists.

---

# 42. Model–Observation Disagreement

When models and observations disagree, the programme should not automatically assume that either side is wrong.

Possible explanations include:

* observational uncertainty;
* short observational periods;
* internal variability;
* model bias;
* spatial mismatch;
* variable-definition mismatch;
* forcing differences;
* resolution;
* sampling differences.

Model–observation disagreement should therefore become a research question rather than an automatic verdict.

---

# 43. Reconstruction–Observation Disagreement

Similarly, disagreements between reconstructions and direct observations may reflect:

* calibration;
* different target variables;
* temporal smoothing;
* spatial representation;
* proxy limitations;
* short overlapping periods.

A reconstruction should be evaluated according to what it was designed to reconstruct.

---

# 44. Dataset Compatibility Is Part of Evidence Evaluation

Two datasets may both be scientifically valid and still be inappropriate to combine.

Compatibility requires consideration of:

* variable definition;
* methodology;
* coordinate system;
* spatial domain;
* temporal domain;
* sampling;
* units;
* uncertainty;
* processing.

Evidence quality and evidence compatibility are therefore separate questions.

---

# 45. Absence of Evidence

Failure to detect a signal does not automatically prove that the phenomenon is absent.

Non-detection may result from:

* insufficient record length;
* limited statistical power;
* noisy observations;
* inadequate spatial coverage;
* inappropriate methods;
* genuinely absent signal.

The project will distinguish:

> **evidence of absence**

from:

> **absence of sufficient evidence.**

---

# 46. Evidence Evaluation Before Synthesis

Before multiple studies are synthesised into a project conclusion, each should be evaluated individually.

The programme should avoid moving directly from:

> many papers

to:

> scientific consensus.

The intermediate work includes:

* source classification;
* methodological evaluation;
* dependency mapping;
* uncertainty assessment;
* comparison;
* conflict analysis.

Only then should synthesis occur.

---

# 47. Project Evidence Synthesis

When the programme eventually synthesises evidence for a major conclusion, the synthesis should state:

1. **The claim being evaluated**
2. **Evidence supporting it**
3. **Evidence challenging it**
4. **Evidence types involved**
5. **Degree of independence**
6. **Major uncertainties**
7. **Areas of agreement**
8. **Areas of disagreement**
9. **Alternative explanations**
10. **Current project confidence**
11. **What additional evidence could change the conclusion**

This structure makes the reasoning behind a conclusion visible.

---

# 48. Evidence Evaluation Is Revisable

Evidence strength may change when:

* observational records lengthen;
* new datasets appear;
* methods improve;
* studies are corrected;
* model generations change;
* new reconstructions are published;
* previously independent evidence is discovered to share dependencies.

Evidence evaluation must therefore be versionable.

A source does not receive one permanent scientific score.

Its relevance and strength depend on the claim and the state of knowledge at the time of evaluation.

---

# 49. Programme Evidence Principle

The evidence framework can be summarised as:

> **Do not ask which source is highest in a universal hierarchy. Ask which evidence is most capable of answering the specific scientific question, how reliable it is, how independent it is, what uncertainty it carries, and whether other evidence converges or disagrees.**

This principle will guide:

* literature review;
* dataset selection;
* model evaluation;
* interpretation;
* research-gap analysis;
* scientific communication.

---

# 50. Relationship to the Research Workflow

[`0.2.1 — Research Workflow`](0.2.1-research-workflow.md) established the sequence through which project investigations proceed.

Evidence evaluation operates throughout that workflow.

It affects:

> **Question → Evidence Map → Research Design → Dataset Selection → Analysis → Comparison → Interpretation → Claim**

Weak evidence at an early stage cannot be repaired merely by applying more sophisticated analysis later.

A technically advanced method cannot manufacture evidential strength that the underlying data do not contain.

---

# 51. Relationship to Scientific Reasoning

The next document:

> **0.2.3 — Scientific Reasoning**

will define how the programme reasons from evidence toward conclusions.

This distinction is important.

`0.2.2` asks:

> **What evidence do we have, and how strong is it for this claim?**

`0.2.3` will ask:

> **What are we logically justified in concluding from that evidence?**

Evidence evaluation and reasoning are related.

They are not the same operation.

---

## Closing Statement

The AMOC Research Program will not treat scientific evidence as a collection of citations arranged behind a conclusion.

Evidence has structure.

It has provenance.

It has uncertainty.

It may be direct or indirect.

It may be independent or dependent.

It may agree, conflict, or remain inconclusive.

Its value depends on the question being asked.

The purpose of evidence evaluation is therefore not to eliminate scientific uncertainty.

It is to determine what the available evidence genuinely allows the programme to say.

---

## Methodological References

The framework in this document is project-specific but is informed by established scientific assessment principles.

### Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change

The IPCC framework for uncertainty assessment evaluates findings through the nature and amount of evidence, the degree of agreement, confidence, and quantitative likelihood where appropriate. It also emphasises traceability between assessment findings and their underlying evidence.

* **IPCC.** *Guidance Note for Lead Authors of the IPCC Fifth Assessment Report on Consistent Treatment of Uncertainties.*
* **IPCC Working Group I.** *Climate Change 2021: The Physical Science Basis, Chapter 1 — Framing, Context and Methods.*

These sources inform the programme’s distinction among evidence, agreement, confidence, and likelihood.

### National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine

* **National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine. (2019).** *Reproducibility and Replicability in Science.*

This source provides broader methodological context concerning scientific verification, reproducibility, and the importance of making scientific results open to checking and recomputation.

These methodological references do not establish a universal evidence-ranking system for the AMOC Research Program. The evidence framework defined above remains a programme-specific implementation designed for the scientific questions addressed by this project.
