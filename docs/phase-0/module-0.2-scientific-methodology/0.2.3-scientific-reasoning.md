# 0.2.3 — Scientific Reasoning

**Phase:** 0 — Research Framework
**Module:** 0.2 — Scientific Methodology
**Status:** Initial publication
**Version:** 0.1.0
**Published:** 16 August 2026
**Author:** Günışığı Aydoğan

> **Publication note:** This document defines the reasoning standards of the AMOC Research Program. It establishes how the programme will move from questions and evidence toward hypotheses, interpretations, and scientific claims. It does not prescribe a single universal philosophy of science. Different research questions may require different forms of reasoning, but the inferential path from evidence to conclusion should remain explicit and open to challenge.

---

## Scientific Reasoning

Scientific research is not completed when evidence has been collected.

Evidence must still be interpreted.

That interpretation requires reasoning.

The same dataset can sometimes support:

* several possible explanations;
* different statistical models;
* competing physical interpretations;
* different degrees of confidence.

The role of scientific reasoning is therefore to determine what conclusions are justified by the available evidence and which conclusions go beyond it.

The AMOC Research Program will distinguish carefully among:

> **observation → pattern → hypothesis → mechanism → interpretation → claim**

These stages are related.

They are not interchangeable.

The central reasoning principle of the programme is:

> **The strength of a conclusion must not exceed the combined strength of the evidence, methodology, and reasoning that support it.**

---

# 1. Reasoning Begins With the Research Question

Scientific reasoning should remain connected to the question being investigated.

Before interpreting evidence, the programme should ask:

1. What exactly is the research question?
2. What type of answer is being sought?
3. What evidence would support that answer?
4. What evidence would challenge it?
5. Which alternative explanations exist?
6. What conclusions would remain unjustified even if the expected result appears?

This reduces the risk of discovering an interesting pattern and retroactively inventing a research question for it.

---

# 2. Observation Is Not Interpretation

The programme will distinguish between what is observed and what is inferred.

For example:

> A measured or derived AMOC transport value changed during a defined observational interval.

may be an observational result.

Statements such as:

> the AMOC is undergoing long-term anthropogenic weakening;

or:

> the circulation is approaching a tipping point;

require additional reasoning and evidence.

The inferential distance between observation and interpretation should remain visible.

---

# 3. Result Is Not Explanation

A statistical result describes something found in an analysis.

An explanation proposes why it occurred.

For example:

### Result

A time series exhibits increasing variance.

### Possible interpretation

The system may be experiencing reduced resilience.

### Alternative interpretations

The pattern may instead reflect:

* changing forcing;
* non-stationarity;
* measurement changes;
* preprocessing;
* limited record length;
* stochastic variability.

The programme will therefore resist the progression:

> **pattern detected → mechanism confirmed**

unless the intermediate reasoning has been demonstrated.

---

# 4. Scientific Hypotheses

A scientific hypothesis proposes an explanation, relationship, or expected behaviour that can be evaluated against evidence.

A useful hypothesis should be:

* sufficiently specific;
* connected to observable consequences;
* physically meaningful;
* open to challenge;
* capable of being weakened by evidence.

The project should avoid hypotheses that are so flexible that every possible outcome can be interpreted as support.

---

## 4.1 Hypotheses Must Permit Failure

Where appropriate, a hypothesis should allow the possibility that the evidence will not support it.

The project should ask:

> **What observation or analytical result would make us less confident in this hypothesis?**

If no plausible evidence could weaken the hypothesis, it is poorly suited to empirical evaluation.

---

# 5. Scientific and Statistical Hypotheses

Scientific hypotheses and statistical hypotheses should not be confused.

A scientific hypothesis may concern:

* circulation dynamics;
* freshwater forcing;
* density structure;
* variability;
* resilience;
* physical mechanisms.

A statistical hypothesis concerns a measurable statistical property.

For example:

### Scientific hypothesis

A physical process contributes to changes in a circulation metric.

### Statistical hypothesis

A relationship between two measured variables differs from zero under a defined model.

Rejecting a statistical null hypothesis does not automatically establish the scientific mechanism.

---

# 6. Multiple Competing Hypotheses

Where several explanations are plausible, the programme should consider them explicitly.

For example, an observed change might potentially reflect:

* anthropogenic forcing;
* natural variability;
* atmospheric forcing;
* freshwater input;
* measurement changes;
* methodological artefacts;
* interacting processes.

The research question should therefore sometimes be framed as:

> **Which explanations remain consistent with the available evidence?**

rather than:

> **How can we prove explanation A?**

---

# 7. Alternative Explanations

Every major interpretation should undergo an alternative-explanation check.

The project should ask:

1. Could another physical process produce this pattern?
2. Could a shared driver explain both variables?
3. Could the result be caused by preprocessing?
4. Could measurement changes produce the signal?
5. Could record length create the apparent relationship?
6. Could the result arise from internal variability?
7. Could model assumptions generate the behaviour?
8. Could the result disappear under another reasonable method?

A conclusion becomes stronger when reasonable alternatives are constrained.

---

# 8. Falsification as a Research Discipline

The programme will not treat falsification as a simplistic rule that one failed test permanently destroys every scientific idea.

Complex Earth-system hypotheses may depend on:

* scale;
* boundary conditions;
* measurement uncertainty;
* model structure;
* multiple interacting mechanisms.

However, the programme will use falsification as a discipline:

> **actively identify evidence that could weaken the preferred interpretation.**

Research should therefore include attempts to challenge, not merely support, its hypotheses.

---

# 9. Confirmation Is Not Enough

A result being consistent with a hypothesis does not necessarily mean that the hypothesis is uniquely supported.

Several hypotheses may predict similar observations.

The programme should distinguish:

### Confirmation

Evidence is compatible with a hypothesis.

### Discrimination

Evidence helps distinguish that hypothesis from competing explanations.

Discriminating evidence is generally more informative.

---

# 10. Deductive Reasoning

Deductive reasoning proceeds from general premises toward consequences that should follow if those premises are true.

In the programme, deductive reasoning may appear when:

* physical theory predicts observable behaviour;
* a mathematical model implies a relationship;
* a hypothesis generates testable consequences.

A simplified structure is:

> If mechanism A operates under conditions B,
> then outcome C should be observable.

The project can then investigate whether outcome C appears.

However, observing C does not necessarily prove A because other mechanisms may also produce C.

---

# 11. Inductive Reasoning

Inductive reasoning proceeds from observations toward broader generalisations.

For example:

Repeated observations may suggest a recurring relationship or pattern.

Induction is essential to empirical science, but conclusions remain probabilistic rather than logically guaranteed.

The programme will therefore avoid treating:

> repeated historical behaviour

as equivalent to:

> certain future behaviour.

---

# 12. Abductive Reasoning

Abductive reasoning asks which explanation best accounts for the available evidence.

This form of reasoning is especially relevant when:

* several explanations are possible;
* direct causal experiments are impossible;
* Earth-system processes interact;
* evidence comes from multiple sources.

The preferred explanation should ideally account for:

* more observations;
* fewer unsupported assumptions;
* known physical mechanisms;
* competing evidence;
* uncertainty.

The “best explanation” remains provisional when alternatives cannot be ruled out.

---

# 13. Mechanistic Reasoning

Mechanistic reasoning asks how a proposed process could physically produce the observed result.

For AMOC research, this may involve consideration of:

* density;
* buoyancy;
* pressure gradients;
* wind forcing;
* freshwater forcing;
* convection;
* water-mass transformation;
* boundary currents;
* ocean–atmosphere interaction.

Mechanistic plausibility provides important support for interpretation.

However:

> **plausible mechanism ≠ demonstrated mechanism.**

A mechanism may be physically possible without being the dominant explanation for the observed phenomenon.

---

# 14. Causal Reasoning

Causal claims require stronger reasoning than associational claims.

The programme will distinguish:

### Association

Two variables change together.

### Temporal relationship

One variable changes before another.

### Mechanistic relationship

A physically credible process links them.

### Causal attribution

Evidence supports the conclusion that changes in one process contributed materially to changes in another.

Correlation alone does not establish causality.

---

# 15. Common Causes

Two variables may correlate because both respond to a third process.

Potential common drivers in climate research may include:

* atmospheric circulation;
* external forcing;
* internal variability;
* freshwater changes;
* ocean–atmosphere interactions.

Therefore:

> **A correlates with B**

does not establish:

> **A causes B.**

The programme should investigate plausible common causes before adopting direct causal interpretations.

---

# 16. Temporal Order

Temporal order can contribute to causal reasoning.

If A is proposed to cause B, then changes in A should normally precede or occur consistently with the timescale required for B to respond.

However, temporal precedence alone is insufficient.

A leading signal can still arise from:

* shared forcing;
* autocorrelation;
* filtering;
* chance;
* lagged responses to another process.

Temporal structure should therefore be combined with physical reasoning.

---

# 17. Counterfactual Reasoning

A causal question often implicitly asks:

> **What would have happened if the proposed driver had been different?**

Direct counterfactual experiments are generally impossible for the real climate system.

Models, natural experiments, ensembles, and statistical methods may sometimes provide approximations.

The programme should clearly label counterfactual conclusions as model- or method-dependent where appropriate.

---

# 18. Detection Is Not Attribution

The programme will maintain a strict distinction between:

### Detection

Evidence that a meaningful change has occurred.

### Attribution

Evidence concerning the causes of that change.

A statistically detectable AMOC trend does not automatically establish its cause.

Attribution requires additional evidence.

---

# 19. Prediction Is Not Explanation

A model may predict a variable accurately without representing the true underlying mechanism.

This is especially important for machine learning.

High predictive performance may demonstrate:

> useful statistical information exists.

It does not necessarily demonstrate:

> the model has discovered the physical causal structure.

Predictive and explanatory objectives should therefore be defined separately.

---

# 20. Exploratory Reasoning

Exploratory research is used to discover:

* patterns;
* relationships;
* anomalies;
* candidate hypotheses;
* possible mechanisms.

Exploration is scientifically valuable.

The methodological danger arises when exploratory discoveries are later presented as though they had been predicted in advance.

The programme will therefore label exploratory findings explicitly.

---

# 21. Confirmatory Reasoning

Confirmatory research evaluates previously specified expectations or hypotheses.

Where feasible, the programme should define before analysis:

* research question;
* variables;
* period;
* preprocessing;
* method;
* decision criteria;
* sensitivity tests.

Confirmatory reasoning is strongest when the analytical choices were not selected after examining the desired result.

---

# 22. Hypothesis Generation and Hypothesis Testing

The programme will distinguish:

> **data used to generate an idea**

from:

> **evidence used to test that idea.**

When the same dataset is used for both, the limitation should be acknowledged.

Where possible, later work may test exploratory findings through:

* independent periods;
* independent datasets;
* alternative methods;
* replication;
* new observations.

---

# 23. Confirmation Bias

Researchers naturally pay more attention to information consistent with their expectations.

The programme will reduce this risk by requiring consideration of:

* contradictory studies;
* negative results;
* alternative hypotheses;
* methodological sensitivity;
* evidence that would weaken the preferred explanation.

The question should be:

> **What does the evidence support?**

not:

> **How can the evidence support what we expected?**

---

# 24. Motivated Reasoning

Climate research can intersect with:

* public concern;
* politics;
* activism;
* risk;
* professional incentives;
* personal beliefs.

Scientific reasoning must remain capable of producing conclusions that are:

* reassuring;
* concerning;
* uncertain;
* inconvenient;
* contrary to prior expectations.

The desirability of a conclusion is irrelevant to its evidential strength.

---

# 25. Cherry-Picking

Cherry-picking occurs when evidence is selected because it supports a preferred conclusion while relevant contradictory evidence is ignored.

The programme will reduce this risk through:

* structured literature review;
* evidence maps;
* documented search strategies;
* conflicting-evidence records;
* sensitivity analysis;
* transparent exclusions.

Evidence should be excluded for scientific reasons, not narrative convenience.

---

# 26. HARKing

Hypothesising After the Results are Known can occur when a result discovered during exploration is presented as though it had been predicted beforehand.

The programme will avoid disguising exploratory discoveries.

A hypothesis generated after analysis is still useful.

It should simply be labelled:

> **hypothesis generated from exploratory analysis**

and tested independently where possible.

---

# 27. Researcher Degrees of Freedom

Analytical results can depend on choices concerning:

* start date;
* end date;
* baseline;
* smoothing;
* filtering;
* detrending;
* variable definitions;
* exclusions;
* statistical models;
* parameter settings.

These choices create researcher degrees of freedom.

The programme will address this through:

* documented decisions;
* predetermined choices where appropriate;
* sensitivity analysis;
* alternative specifications.

A conclusion that only appears under one narrow analytical configuration should be described accordingly.

---

# 28. Multiple Comparisons

Searching many variables, periods, models, or tests increases the possibility of apparently important results arising by chance.

The programme should document when analyses involve substantial multiple testing.

Appropriate statistical treatment may be required during later analytical phases.

Exploratory searching should not be disguised as a single pre-specified test.

---

# 29. Statistical Significance

Statistical significance alone will never be treated as sufficient evidence of scientific importance.

A statistically significant result may still be:

* physically trivial;
* method-dependent;
* too small to matter;
* driven by confounding;
* sensitive to record length;
* inconsistent with mechanism.

The programme will consider:

* effect magnitude;
* uncertainty;
* robustness;
* physical meaning;
* relevance.

---

# 30. Non-Significance

Failure to reach statistical significance does not automatically prove that no relationship exists.

Possible reasons include:

* weak effect;
* short record;
* large variability;
* measurement uncertainty;
* insufficient statistical power;
* inappropriate method.

The programme will avoid converting:

> **not detected**

into:

> **does not exist**

without justification.

---

# 31. Practical and Physical Significance

The programme will distinguish among:

* statistical significance;
* practical significance;
* physical significance.

A result may be statistically detectable but physically negligible.

Another result may be physically important but difficult to establish statistically because the available record is short.

Interpretation should consider all three.

---

# 32. Uncertainty in Reasoning

Uncertainty applies not only to measurements.

It also applies to interpretation.

The programme may encounter uncertainty concerning:

* mechanism;
* causality;
* model applicability;
* reconstruction validity;
* alternative explanations.

Interpretive uncertainty should be stated explicitly.

---

# 33. Bayesian Updating as a General Principle

The programme will adopt the general scientific principle that confidence should change as evidence changes.

This does not require every analysis to use formal Bayesian statistics.

Conceptually:

> prior understanding + new evidence → revised confidence.

New evidence may:

* strengthen;
* weaken;
* complicate;
* overturn;
* leave unchanged;

an existing interpretation.

Confidence should therefore remain revisable.

---

# 34. Converging Evidence

Confidence may increase when different independent lines of evidence support the same interpretation.

Particularly useful convergence may involve combinations of:

* observations;
* theory;
* reconstructions;
* models;
* independent datasets;
* different analytical methods.

Convergence is most valuable when the evidence sources do not share identical weaknesses.

---

# 35. Triangulation

Triangulation involves examining a question through multiple methods or evidence types.

For example, a conclusion might be explored using:

* observations;
* physical calculations;
* statistical analysis;
* model comparison;
* published literature.

Triangulation can reduce dependence on a single method.

However, combining multiple weak or dependent sources does not automatically create strong evidence.

---

# 36. Parsimony

When multiple explanations account for the evidence equally well, simpler explanations may deserve preference.

However, simplicity should not be treated as proof.

Earth-system processes may genuinely be complex.

The programme will therefore use parsimony as a reasoning preference rather than an absolute rule.

---

# 37. Consilience

A scientific interpretation becomes stronger when evidence from different domains fits together coherently.

For example:

* physical theory;
* observation;
* statistical behaviour;
* model experiments;

may collectively support an interpretation.

The programme will seek such coherence while remaining alert to shared assumptions and dependencies.

---

# 38. Contradictions

Contradictory evidence should trigger investigation rather than immediate rejection.

The programme should ask:

* Are the variables actually the same?
* Are the timescales different?
* Are spatial domains different?
* Are methods different?
* Are uncertainty ranges overlapping?
* Do datasets share dependencies?
* Does one study test a different hypothesis?

Many apparent scientific contradictions are actually differences in definitions or research design.

Real contradictions should remain visible when unresolved.

---

# 39. Null Results

Null results can provide scientifically useful information.

They may:

* constrain effect size;
* challenge a hypothesis;
* reveal insufficient evidence;
* expose methodological limitations.

The programme will not treat null results as failed research merely because they produce an unexciting graph.

---

# 40. Negative Evidence

Evidence that weakens a preferred interpretation should receive the same methodological attention as evidence that supports it.

The programme should actively preserve:

* contradictory findings;
* failed predictions;
* unsuccessful replications;
* sensitivity problems.

Scientific confidence should reflect the entire evidence landscape.

---

# 41. Anomaly Does Not Equal Discovery

Unexpected observations may indicate:

* new science;
* instrument problems;
* processing errors;
* metadata errors;
* unusual but valid events;
* random variation.

An anomaly is a question.

It is not automatically a discovery.

The first response should be investigation.

---

# 42. Extraordinary Claims

Claims with major scientific implications require evidence commensurate with their strength.

For example, a claim of:

* confirmed tipping;
* imminent collapse;
* precise collapse timing;
* newly discovered causal mechanism;

requires substantially stronger evidence than a descriptive finding.

The more consequential the claim, the more carefully alternative explanations and uncertainty should be evaluated.

---

# 43. Reasoning About Models

Models should be interpreted according to what they are capable of supporting.

A model may provide evidence concerning:

* mechanism;
* sensitivity;
* scenario response;
* plausibility;
* comparative behaviour.

It does not automatically provide evidence that:

* the real system will behave identically;
* one simulated pathway is inevitable;
* model agreement eliminates structural uncertainty.

Reasoning from models to reality requires explicit justification.

---

# 44. Ensemble Reasoning

Model ensembles may provide information about:

* variability;
* uncertainty;
* robustness;
* scenario spread.

However, ensemble members are not necessarily independent samples from reality.

The programme will avoid interpreting ensemble frequency naively as real-world probability unless scientifically justified.

---

# 45. Reasoning Across Timescales

Relationships observed at one timescale may not apply at another.

For example:

* seasonal variability;
* interannual variability;
* decadal variability;
* centennial change;

may involve different mechanisms or relative contributions.

The programme will avoid extrapolating short-term relationships automatically into long-term conclusions.

---

# 46. Reasoning Across Spatial Scales

A measurement at one latitude or region does not automatically represent the entire Atlantic overturning circulation.

Spatial interpretation should consider:

* observing location;
* circulation structure;
* coherence;
* propagation;
* boundary processes;
* regional differences.

Local evidence requires justification before supporting basin-wide claims.

---

# 47. Reasoning From Proxy Evidence

Proxy evidence involves an inferential chain.

The programme should make that chain explicit:

> **proxy measurement → reconstructed variable → physical interpretation → AMOC-related inference**

Uncertainty can enter at each step.

Long inferential chains are not automatically invalid.

They require greater transparency.

---

# 48. Reasoning From Reanalysis

Reanalysis provides highly useful physically structured information.

However, reasoning should recognise that reanalysis combines:

* observations;
* numerical models;
* assimilation methods.

Therefore, statements derived from reanalysis should not be presented as though every value were directly observed.

---

# 49. Reasoning About Early-Warning Signals

Early-warning indicators require particularly careful inference.

A pattern such as increasing autocorrelation may be:

* theoretically consistent with critical slowing down;

without establishing:

* that the real AMOC is approaching a tipping point.

Reasoning must consider:

* theoretical assumptions;
* record length;
* forcing;
* preprocessing;
* alternative mechanisms;
* indicator sensitivity;
* model validation.

The programme will not collapse the reasoning chain:

> **indicator → critical slowing down → tipping → imminent collapse**

into a single conclusion.

Each arrow requires evidence.

---

# 50. Reasoning About Absence of Collapse

The opposite error must also be avoided.

Failure to establish evidence of imminent collapse does not demonstrate that:

* the AMOC cannot collapse;
* no tipping mechanism exists;
* future forcing poses no risk.

The programme will distinguish between:

> **current evidence does not establish X**

and:

> **X is impossible.**

---

# 51. Claim Calibration

Project language should correspond to evidential strength.

Possible formulations include:

### Direct description

* “The dataset shows…”
* “The analysis calculates…”

### Moderate inference

* “The result is consistent with…”
* “The evidence supports…”

### Limited inference

* “The result suggests…”
* “The evidence provides limited support…”

### Uncertainty

* “The available evidence cannot distinguish…”
* “The interpretation remains uncertain…”

### Negative conclusion

* “The analysis does not support…”

### Boundary statement

* “This result does not establish…”

Precise language is part of scientific reasoning.

---

# 52. Reasoning Record

For major findings, the programme may eventually maintain a structured reasoning record containing:

* Claim ID
* Research question
* Observed result
* Proposed interpretation
* Supporting evidence
* Contradictory evidence
* Alternative explanations
* Physical mechanism
* Assumptions
* Uncertainty
* Sensitivity
* Confidence level
* Claim boundaries
* Revision history

This would make inferential reasoning inspectable rather than leaving it hidden inside prose.

---

# 53. Claim Graphs

As the project becomes more complex, relationships among findings may eventually be represented as claim graphs.

A claim could be linked to:

* supporting datasets;
* papers;
* calculations;
* models;
* assumptions;
* dependent claims;
* contradictory claims.

This would allow the programme to trace how changing one evidence source affects higher-level conclusions.

Such infrastructure is a potential future development rather than a current requirement.

---

# 54. Reasoning Check Before Publication

Before publishing a major interpretation, the programme should ask:

1. What exactly was observed or calculated?
2. What interpretation is being proposed?
3. Which assumptions connect the result to the interpretation?
4. What alternative explanations exist?
5. What evidence would weaken the interpretation?
6. Is the reasoning physically plausible?
7. Is causality being implied?
8. Does the evidence support that causal strength?
9. Does the claim generalise beyond the analysed period or location?
10. Is the language stronger than the evidence?

If these questions reveal unsupported inferential jumps, the conclusion should be revised.

---

# 55. Strong Conclusions Require Multiple Layers

A strong scientific conclusion should ideally have support across several layers:

> **measurement quality → analytical validity → robustness → physical plausibility → independent evidence → literature consistency**

Weakness at one layer may reduce confidence in the final conclusion.

No amount of eloquent interpretation can repair invalid data or analysis.

---

# 56. Reasoning Should Remain Reversible

A scientific conclusion should be capable of being revised when its supporting evidence changes.

The programme should therefore avoid constructing arguments whose structure makes revision psychologically or reputationally difficult.

Conclusions belong to the evidence.

They are not personal possessions.

---

# 57. Degrees of Confidence

The project may express conclusions using qualitative confidence categories when useful.

Possible internal categories include:

### High Confidence

The interpretation is supported by strong, relevant, converging evidence with limited plausible alternatives.

### Moderate Confidence

Evidence supports the interpretation, but important uncertainty or alternatives remain.

### Low Confidence

Evidence is limited, indirect, method-dependent, or substantially uncertain.

### Unresolved

Current evidence does not justify selecting among competing interpretations.

These project categories should not be confused with formally calibrated confidence terminology from external scientific assessments unless explicitly mapped.

---

# 58. Reasoning and Scientific Humility

Scientific humility does not require avoiding conclusions.

It requires recognising the boundaries of those conclusions.

The programme should be comfortable stating:

* we do not know;
* evidence is insufficient;
* two explanations remain plausible;
* the method is too sensitive;
* the observational period is too short;
* the model cannot answer this question;
* the result contradicted our expectation.

These are valid scientific outcomes.

---

# 59. Programme Reasoning Standard

The reasoning framework can be summarised as:

> **Describe what was observed. Identify what is inferred. Make the assumptions connecting them visible. Challenge the preferred interpretation with alternatives. Require stronger evidence for stronger claims. Revise confidence when the evidence changes.**

---

# 60. Relationship to Evidence Evaluation

[`0.2.2 — Evidence Hierarchy and Evaluation`](0.2.2-evidence-hierarchy-and-evaluation.md) established how evidence should be classified and evaluated.

This document defines how the programme reasons from that evidence.

The relationship is:

> **Evidence evaluation asks what we have.**

> **Scientific reasoning asks what it allows us to conclude.**

Strong evidence can still produce a weak conclusion if the reasoning is invalid.

Sound reasoning cannot compensate for inadequate evidence.

Both are required.

---

# 61. Relationship to Reproducibility

The next document:

> **0.2.4 — Reproducibility and Open Science**

will extend this framework by asking whether another researcher can inspect and reproduce the analytical path supporting a conclusion.

Scientific reasoning should therefore eventually be traceable through:

> **question → evidence → analysis → result → interpretation → claim**

Reproducibility will make that path inspectable.

## Closing Statement

The AMOC Research Program will not treat reasoning as the invisible space between a graph and a conclusion.

That space is part of the research.

An observation does not automatically reveal its cause.

A statistical relationship does not automatically establish a physical mechanism.

A model result does not automatically predict reality.

An early-warning indicator does not automatically establish tipping.

And uncertainty does not automatically mean that nothing can be concluded.

Scientific reasoning is the process of determining how far the evidence actually allows the project to move.

The programme will therefore make that movement visible, challengeable, and revisable.
