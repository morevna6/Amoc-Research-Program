# AMOC Research Program

**A scientific, modular, and reproducible investigation of the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation**

> **Project status:** Active
> **Current phase:** Phase 0 — Research Framework
> **Current module:** Module 0.1 — Project Philosophy
> **Initial release:** 28 July 2026

## Overview

The **AMOC Research Program** is a long-term independent research initiative dedicated to understanding the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation through scientific literature, authoritative observational datasets, reproducible data analysis, physical reasoning, and careful scientific communication.

This repository is not intended to be a one-time dashboard project or a collection of disconnected analyses.

It is being developed as a living research record in which the scientific foundations, source decisions, datasets, assumptions, methods, code, limitations, revisions, and conclusions remain traceable.

The project does **not** begin with the assumption that the AMOC is stable, collapsing, or approaching a predetermined tipping date.

Competing findings and unresolved uncertainties will be documented rather than forced into a single narrative.

## Why This Project Exists

The Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation, commonly referred to as the **AMOC**, is a major component of the Earth’s climate system.

It contributes to the redistribution of heat, freshwater, salt, carbon, nutrients, and other ocean properties throughout the Atlantic Ocean. Changes in its strength and structure may influence regional climate, precipitation patterns, dynamic sea level, marine ecosystems, coastal systems, and long-term societal resilience.

Despite its importance, the AMOC remains difficult to observe, reconstruct, and predict.

Continuous basin-wide monitoring at approximately 26.5°N began only in 2004 through the RAPID observing system. This record has transformed scientific understanding of short-term and interannual AMOC variability, but it remains short compared with the decadal and multidecadal timescales on which the circulation may vary.

Understanding the AMOC therefore requires the combined use of:

* direct observations;
* hydrographic measurements;
* ocean reanalyses;
* palaeoclimate evidence;
* statistical indicators;
* theoretical models;
* climate-model simulations;
* scenario-dependent projections.

Each type of evidence contains different assumptions, uncertainties, spatial limitations, temporal limitations, and methodological biases.

This project exists to examine those differences through a long-term, modular, transparent, and reproducible research programme.

## Research Questions

Every module will return to four guiding questions:

1. **What do we currently know?**
2. **How do we know it?**
3. **How certain are we?**
4. **What remains unknown?**

These questions will guide the project from foundational scientific study to literature review, dataset selection, data engineering, exploratory analysis, physical calculation, modelling, interpretation, and communication.

---

## Core Principles

The project follows several foundational principles:

* **Evidence before narrative**
* **Research before visualisation**
* **Reproducibility before convenience**
* **Transparent assumptions and limitations**
* **Clear distinction between observations, reconstructions, simulations, and projections**
* **Versioned conclusions that may change when evidence changes**
* **Accessible communication without sacrificing scientific accuracy**
* **Machine learning only where scientifically justified**
* **Uncertainty treated as part of the result**
* **No predetermined conclusion**

The project will not select evidence according to a preferred narrative.

Where findings conflict, disagreement will be documented rather than silently removed.

Where evidence is incomplete, uncertainty will be preserved rather than converted into false precision.

## Scientific and Computational Approach

The project connects scientific understanding with computational practice.

Data analysis cannot be scientifically meaningful when detached from the physical system that produced the data.

Before calculating trends, constructing indicators, or applying predictive models, the project will establish foundations in:

* Earth system science;
* climate dynamics;
* oceanography;
* fluid dynamics;
* ocean thermodynamics;
* water-mass formation;
* Atlantic circulation;
* AMOC observation systems;
* uncertainty analysis;
* the historical development of AMOC research.

Every dataset, equation, transformation, visualisation, and model should eventually answer five questions:

1. What physical quantity does it represent?
2. How was it measured, reconstructed, or simulated?
3. What assumptions were required?
4. What uncertainty and limitations does it contain?
5. What conclusions can it legitimately support?

## Project Roadmap

The research programme is organised into the following phases.

### Phase 0 — Research Framework

Establish the project philosophy, scientific methodology, evidence standards, reproducibility principles, documentation practices, citation standards, and literature-management system.

### Phase I — Foundations of the Earth System

Develop the theoretical background required to understand Earth as a coupled system.

### Phase II — Ocean Physics

Study the physical principles governing ocean circulation, including density, buoyancy, pressure gradients, Coriolis force, mixing, stratification, convection, and ocean thermodynamics.

### Phase III — Thermohaline Circulation

Understand the global overturning circulation, heat and salt transport, and deep-water formation.

### Phase IV — Atlantic Ocean System

Study the Atlantic Ocean, its gyres, currents, high-latitude seas, and freshwater sources.

### Phase V — AMOC

Develop a complete theoretical understanding of the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation.

### Phase VI — Observation Systems

Study how the AMOC is observed through RAPID, OSNAP, MOVE, SAMBA, Argo floats, hydrographic sections, moorings, satellites, reanalyses, and climate models.

### Phase VII — Historical Development

Examine the evolution of scientific understanding, from early theories and Stommel’s work to modern observing systems and numerical models.

### Phase VIII — Current Scientific Understanding

Review current evidence, competing hypotheses, confidence levels, uncertainties, areas of agreement, and areas of disagreement.

### Phase IX — Research Gap Analysis

Identify unanswered scientific and methodological questions.

### Phase X — Data Infrastructure

Build a reproducible system for data acquisition, validation, metadata management, versioning, storage, and quality control.

### Phase XI — Exploratory Data Analysis

Assess data quality, missing values, outliers, temporal coverage, spatial coverage, distributions, and correlations.

### Phase XII — Scientific Analysis

Conduct statistical and physical analysis, including:

* density computation;
* heat transport;
* freshwater transport;
* time-series analysis;
* trend analysis;
* spectral analysis;
* variance analysis;
* autocorrelation;
* early-warning signals;
* critical slowing down;
* change-point detection.

### Phase XIII — Computational Modelling

Develop mathematical models, numerical simulations, reconstructions, scenario analyses, sensitivity analyses, and machine-learning methods where scientifically justified.

### Phase XIV — Interpretation

Interpret results physically, compare them with published literature, validate findings, document limitations, and consider alternative explanations.

### Phase XV — Global Impacts

Investigate possible relationships with:

* European climate;
* North American climate;
* sea level;
* agriculture;
* fisheries;
* biodiversity;
* the carbon cycle;
* weather extremes;
* infrastructure;
* economy;
* society.

### Phase XVI — Scientific Communication

Develop:

* interactive dashboards;
* static scientific figures;
* infographics;
* educational animations;
* technical documentation;
* website articles;
* LinkedIn articles;
* public presentations.

### Phase XVII — Future Research

Document open questions, new datasets, potential collaborations, methodological improvements, and future directions.

See the complete [Research Roadmap](ROADMAP.md).

## Current Boundaries

At this stage, the repository contains the research framework rather than completed scientific analysis.

The project does not currently claim:

* a new estimate of AMOC strength;
* detection of an AMOC tipping point;
* a forecast of collapse timing;
* confirmation or rejection of a specific published study;
* a complete synthesis of AMOC literature;
* a validated early-warning signal;
* a production-ready data pipeline;
* a finished scientific model.

Such claims may only be made later if supported by appropriate evidence, methods, validation, and uncertainty assessment.

---

## Scientific Communication Policy

The project will avoid using dramatic language as a substitute for evidence.

It will also avoid using scientific uncertainty as an excuse for indifference.

Scientific caution does not mean that a risk is unimportant. It means that claims must remain proportional to the evidence supporting them.

The project will distinguish carefully among:

* short-term variability;
* long-term weakening;
* resilience loss;
* critical slowing down;
* threshold crossing;
* tipping risk;
* partial circulation reorganisation;
* complete AMOC collapse.

These concepts are related, but they are not interchangeable.

## Project Philosophy

This project is not built around the promise of certainty.

It is built around the commitment to investigate uncertainty honestly.

The AMOC is scientifically consequential, globally connected, and incompletely understood. Its importance demands sustained attention, while its uncertainty demands methodological discipline.

This repository documents that investigation from its foundations onward.
